If you are resitting GCSE Maths this November, ten weeks can feel very short.

It is.

But ten focused weeks can still make a real difference, especially if you were close to the grade you needed in the summer.

The aim is not to relearn every topic from Year 10 and Year 11.

It is to find where you are losing marks, fix those areas, and spend enough time on exam-style questions that the November papers no longer feel unfamiliar.

## When are the November 2026 GCSE Maths exams?

For AQA GCSE Mathematics, the November 2026 papers are scheduled for:

- **Paper 1:** 4 November 2026
- **Paper 2:** 6 November 2026
- **Paper 3:** 9 November 2026

Each paper lasts 1 hour 30 minutes.

Pearson Edexcel GCSE Maths follows the same 4, 6 and 9 November schedule for its November 2026 series.

That gives students starting in late August roughly ten weeks to prepare.

## Before Week 1: Find out what went wrong

Do not begin by opening a revision guide at page one.

Look at your summer result and ask:

- Which topics did I struggle with?
- Did I lose marks through weak Maths or poor exam technique?
- Was timing a problem?
- Did I make repeated calculator or arithmetic mistakes?
- Was I close to the next grade?

## Weeks 1–2: Repair the basics

Start with the topics that affect many other parts of GCSE Maths.

These may include:

- fractions, decimals and percentages
- negative numbers
- ratio
- basic algebra
- solving equations
- number operations

Do targeted questions rather than full papers.

At this stage, accuracy matters more than speed.

## Weeks 3–4: Attack your weakest topics

Now move into the areas that cost you the most marks.

For one student, that may be algebra.

For another, it may be geometry, graphs or probability.

Use a simple pattern:

**Learn → practise → mark → correct → repeat**

Do not move on simply because you understand the worked example.

Move on when you can answer similar questions independently.

## Weeks 5–6: Mix the topics

This is where revision starts becoming more exam-like.

GCSE Maths papers do not tell you:

**“This is a ratio question.”**

You have to recognise the method yourself.

Start completing mixed-topic sets and combine easier questions with more demanding ones.

Keep a mistake log and record why each error happened.

## Weeks 7–8: Start full past papers

Now begin timed past papers.

Do not focus only on your final score.

After each paper, identify:

- questions you could not start
- questions where you used the wrong method
- careless mistakes
- topics that keep appearing in your error log
- questions where you spent too long

Your corrections are as important as the paper itself.

## Week 9: Work under real exam conditions

By this stage, your practice should feel much closer to the real November exam.

Complete full papers without notes, videos or interruptions.

Practise moving on when a question is taking too long.

Then return to it later.

You should also be comfortable with both non-calculator and calculator work if your exam board requires both.

## Week 10: Consolidate, do not panic

The final week is not the time to suddenly learn twenty new topics.

Focus on:

- recurring mistakes
- important formulas and methods
- weak areas that have improved but still need practice
- a small number of timed questions
- calculator confidence
- getting enough sleep before the exam

You want to arrive at the November papers clear-headed, not exhausted.

## A simple weekly GCSE Maths resit routine

You do not need to revise for hours every night.

A realistic week could look like this:

- **Monday:** weak topic
- **Tuesday:** second weak topic
- **Wednesday:** revisit Monday's topic
- **Thursday:** exam-style mixed questions
- **Friday:** review mistakes
- **Weekend:** longer timed practice

Consistency is more useful than one huge revision session followed by four days of doing nothing.

## Can you move from grade 3 to grade 4 in ten weeks?

Yes, it is possible.

But there is no automatic guarantee.

A student who narrowly missed grade 4 and has relatively small gaps is in a very different position from someone who struggled across most of the specification.

The biggest advantage of a ten-week plan is focus.

You know your previous result.

You know the target.

Now your revision can become much more specific.

## GCSE Maths Resit 2026 FAQs

### Is ten weeks enough to prepare for a GCSE Maths resit?

It can be, especially for students who narrowly missed their target grade and can identify the areas that need improvement.

### How many hours should I revise each week?

There is no perfect number. Regular focused sessions are generally more useful than occasional very long sessions.

### Should I do a past paper every day?

No. Past papers are useful, but early revision should also include targeted work on weak topics.

### When should I start timed papers?

Begin introducing full timed papers once you have strengthened your weakest areas, usually around the middle of the ten-week plan.

### What should I do if I keep making the same mistakes?

Record them. Identify why they keep happening, revisit the underlying topic and practise similar questions until the pattern changes.

## Ten weeks. One clear goal.

Do not spend the next ten weeks trying to become perfect at every part of GCSE Maths.

You do not need perfection.

You need more marks than you earned last time.

Work out where those marks are hiding.

Fix the weak topics.

Practise under pressure.

Learn from every mistake.

And walk into November knowing that your preparation was different this time.
