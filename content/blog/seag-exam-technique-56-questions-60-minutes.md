A child can know the Maths.

They can understand the English.

And they can still lose marks in SEAG because they **run out of time**.

For the 2026 SEAG Entrance Assessment, each main paper contains:

* **28 English questions**
* **28 Maths questions**
* **60 minutes**

That is **56 questions in one hour**.

So good SEAG preparation must include more than learning topics.

Children also need to learn **how to use their time**.

## How much time is available per question?

If you divide 60 minutes by 56 questions, it works out at just over **one minute per question on average**.

But that does **not** mean your child should spend exactly one minute on every question.

Some questions may take only 20 or 30 seconds.

Others will need longer.

The important skill is recognising when a question is taking **too much time**.

## Rule 1: Do not get trapped on one difficult question

One question should never destroy the rest of the paper.

If your child has read a question carefully, tried a sensible approach and is still stuck, they should move on rather than spending several minutes fighting with it.

There may be easier marks waiting later in the paper.

A useful mindset is:

**Do what you can, move on, then return if time allows.**

## Rule 2: Easy marks should be collected efficiently

Children sometimes spend too long checking questions they already know how to answer.

If the question is straightforward:

* read it carefully
* solve it
* check quickly
* move on

The aim is not to rush.

It is to avoid spending two minutes on something that could safely be completed in 40 seconds.

## Rule 3: Read before calculating

Maths word problems are one of the easiest places to waste time.

Children may start adding or multiplying numbers before they have understood the question.

Instead, pause briefly and ask:

**What am I trying to find?**

Then calculate.

Those few seconds can prevent an entire wrong method.

## Rule 4: Do not race through English comprehension

Timing problems can also happen because children rush.

In comprehension, reading too quickly can lead to:

* missing important words
* choosing an answer that sounds right
* overlooking evidence in the passage
* rereading the same section repeatedly

It is often faster to read properly once than to rush and repeatedly go back.

## Rule 5: Practise moving between English and Maths

SEAG Paper 1 and Paper 2 both contain English and Maths within the same paper.

Children therefore need to become comfortable changing from reading and language questions to numerical reasoning.

Mixed practice is important.

Do not make every revision session:

**30 Maths questions only**

or

**30 English questions only**

Include sessions where your child has to switch between different question types.

## Build timing gradually

Do not suddenly give a child a full 60-minute paper and expect perfect pacing.

Start smaller.

For example:

**10 questions:** practise accuracy first.

Then:

**20 questions:** introduce a sensible time target.

Then:

**half a paper:** work on maintaining concentration.

Finally:

**56 questions in 60 minutes:** practise under realistic conditions.

This allows speed to develop without teaching your child to panic.

## Teach your child to recognise time-wasting behaviour

During practice, watch for things such as:

* repeatedly rereading the same question
* spending several minutes on one difficult Maths problem
* checking the same answer three or four times
* becoming upset after one difficult question
* rushing the final section because too much time was used earlier

Those habits matter.

A child who understands the syllabus but repeatedly loses ten minutes through poor decisions needs **exam technique**, not another worksheet.

## What if your child never finishes practice papers?

Do not simply tell them to “work faster”.

Find out **why** they are slow.

Perhaps:

* basic calculations are not fluent enough
* comprehension takes too long
* difficult questions are not being skipped
* they over-check answers
* anxiety causes them to freeze
* they struggle to understand worded problems

Different timing problems need different solutions.

This is why analysing a practice paper is more useful than looking only at the final score.

## Accuracy still matters more than rushing

The goal is not to answer 56 questions as quickly as possible.

It is to answer **as many as possible correctly within the available time**.

A child who races through the first half and makes avoidable mistakes has not improved their exam technique.

Good pacing should feel controlled.

## Practise under real SEAG conditions

As November gets closer, children should complete some full papers with:

* 60 uninterrupted minutes
* no help
* the official answer sheet
* a quiet environment
* corrections afterwards

SEAG Paper 1 takes place on **Saturday 14 November 2026**, followed by Paper 2 on **Saturday 21 November 2026**.

By then, sitting for 60 minutes should not feel unfamiliar.

## Your child does not need to answer every question at the same speed

The strongest exam technique is flexible.

Quick questions should be completed efficiently.

More demanding questions can receive additional time.

Very difficult questions should not be allowed to steal time from easier marks.

That is the skill children should be practising now.
