A parent asks for a number of SAT Math sessions, but two students with the same practice score can need very different plans. One may have a narrow algebra gap; another may lose points across several areas because they cannot translate questions into mathematics.

Before choosing a package, identify what needs teaching and how much practice the learner can sustain. ## Start with a recent independent attempt

Use unfamiliar official practice under the student's appropriate conditions. Keep the original working and mark guesses as well as wrong answers. A correct guess does not establish secure knowledge, while an accurate but very slow solution can reveal a pacing concern.

Group the difficulties by the first wrong decision. Is the student forming the equation incorrectly, mishandling a negative sign or answering for x when the question asks for another expression? The lesson plan should respond to those distinctions.

A broad statement such as needs a higher score does not tell you how many sessions are useful. An initial goal such as interpreting linear models independently is easier to teach and review.

## Separate content teaching from timed rehearsal

A student who cannot solve a problem without a clock may need explanation and prerequisite repair before speed practice. Another who solves fresh questions accurately but gets stuck choosing methods under pressure may need a different emphasis.

The [official SAT structure guide](https://satsuite.collegeboard.org/sat/whats-on-the-test/structure) explains the Math section's two-module arrangement. Preparation should use the current format and the student's approved timing, rather than an old pacing rule applied to every learner.

Our article on [a SAT Math score below 600](sat-maths-score-stuck-below-600-what-to-fix.html) explores why targeted foundations may matter more than repeated full tests. A higher-scoring student may instead need more precise work on a smaller set of decisions.

## Give each lesson a job

One session might teach a relationship, such as how a percentage change is modelled. Independent questions can then check whether the learner selects that model without a worksheet heading announcing it.

A later lesson reviews the attempt and extends the skill or revisits the explanation. This sequence gives a reason for the next appointment. Attending several demonstrations without working alone provides much less information about retention.

If more frequent sessions are proposed, ask what additional purpose they serve. They could separate content repair from mixed practice, but the student still needs realistic time to attempt tasks between them.

## Work backwards from the intended test date

List school commitments, other preparation and available study periods. A plan that assumes hours of practice every evening may be unsuitable for a student already managing a heavy timetable.

Closer to the test, prioritisation becomes more important. It does not make a guaranteed score jump possible. Ask which goals are realistic to work on in the time available and which difficulties may require longer support.

Do not count a familiar paper completed faster on a repeat attempt as decisive proof that the schedule is working. Review changed questions and record testing conditions so that comparisons remain useful.

## Review before extending the commitment

Agree a point to examine independent work and consider the next priorities. Look for fewer repeated errors, more efficient method selection and clearer interpretations alongside practice results. If progress is limited, ask what will change in the teaching rather than automatically purchasing more of the same.
