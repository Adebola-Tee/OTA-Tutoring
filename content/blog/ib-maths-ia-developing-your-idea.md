Your child has chosen basketball, music, architecture or climate as an IB Maths IA topic. They have collected pictures and written an introduction. Then comes the difficult question. What mathematics will the investigation actually do?

An interesting subject is a starting point, not a research question. The next step is to make the idea narrow enough to investigate and mathematically rich enough to explain. Adding advanced formulas before that decision usually creates more confusion.

## Turn the interest into a question

“Mathematics in basketball” is too broad. A more manageable investigation might compare a simplified model of a ball’s trajectory with observations from a particular set of throws. The student then has a question, variables to define and a reason to choose a model.

That is an illustration, not a topic prescription. The student should choose and develop their own investigation, taking account of the school’s guidance and the appropriate course level. A narrow question is useful only if it allows meaningful mathematical work.

Ask three things before collecting more material. What will be calculated or modelled? What information is needed? What would the result help the student understand? If those answers remain vague, work on the question first.

## Build a mathematical route before writing pages

The [IB’s description of the mathematics exploration](https://ibpublishing.ibo.org/exinst/apps/exinst/index.html?chapter=1&doc=EX_instructions_2026_e&part=12) emphasises mathematical communication and thoughtful reflection. That means the report should show decisions and understanding, not just an attractive topic and software output.

Sketch a route on one page. Define the quantities, identify suitable mathematics, try a small calculation and consider what could make the approach unreliable. This rough version helps expose problems while they are still easy to fix.

For a trajectory model, the student might define horizontal and vertical position, explain the chosen equation and examine how well it fits the observations. Assumptions about air resistance, measurement or camera angle would need discussion. The mathematics and the limitations belong together.

## Make every calculation earn its place

A graph pasted from software is not self-explanatory. The learner should explain the axes, variables, model choice and what the graph contributes to answering the question. If regression is used, why is that model appropriate? What happens where it fits poorly?

Similarly, using a difficult technique is not automatically better than using accessible mathematics well. A student needs to understand the method sufficiently to explain its purpose and interpret its result. Mathematics that cannot be explained often becomes decoration.

Encourage the learner to annotate the rough calculations before polishing the report. “This comparison shows…” and “The model becomes less reliable when…” are useful prompts because they connect the work to the investigation.

## Reflection should change the investigation

Reflection is stronger when it happens during the work. Perhaps a small dataset cannot support the original claim. Perhaps the model works in one range and fails elsewhere. That discovery can lead to a narrower conclusion or a better method.

An honest limitation is not a confession that the investigation failed. It shows that the student understands what the mathematics can and cannot establish. Avoid a final paragraph that simply lists generic weaknesses unrelated to the actual calculations.
