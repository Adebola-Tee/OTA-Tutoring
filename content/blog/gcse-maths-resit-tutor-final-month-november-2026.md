**Updated 1 November 2026.** A learner preparing for the November GCSE Maths resits no longer needs a three week revision schedule. They need a short, workable plan for the days before the papers, with enough space to think, practise and rest.

For AQA students, the papers are on 4, 6 and 9 November. Confirm the board and your own arrangements using the [official timetable](https://www.aqa.org.uk/files/cec4fc90-997e-4da1-b3c2-e217c0e58fdb/7f9db72615b207c4a899032ba0c5c37e166fd5f8.pdf). Our [resit dates guide](november-gcse-maths-resit-dates-entry-deadlines-2026.html) explains why paper codes matter. This article is for students whose exam entry is already confirmed.

## Start with errors you can actually explain

Choose a recent paper or mixed question set. Put mistakes into three groups: an idea not understood, a method used inaccurately, and work left unfinished. Each group needs a different response. More speed will not solve a missing concept, and another long explanation may not fix an input error.

Choose two priorities for the next practice block. For example, a student may be able to repair substitution with negative numbers and recognising percentage change. Trying to learn every unfamiliar Higher topic immediately can crowd out marks available from more secure work.

## Repair the decision before repeating the calculation

Consider our original example. A jacket costs £80 before a 15% reduction. Its sale price is 0.85 × 80 = £68. Now reverse the story: the sale price is £68 after the reduction. The original price is 68 ÷ 0.85 = £80.

The useful question is what the £68 represents. Adding 15% to £68 treats the sale price as the original whole. Ask the learner to label the whole and the percentage represented before calculating. Then change the numbers and remove the worked solution.

A correct independent answer to the changed problem gives more useful information than agreement with the tutor's demonstration. Revisit the skill later in a mixed set so the learner must recognise it without a topic heading as a clue.

## Make short practice blocks do different jobs

One block can repair an idea. Another can mix that idea with secure topics. A final block can rehearse decisions under time pressure. These blocks need not be equally long. Stop when concentration has faded enough that the work becomes repeated guessing.

For a student whose arithmetic is secure but whose answers are incomplete, ask for labelled units and a final sentence where appropriate. For one who rushes, practise an estimate and a deliberate reread of the question before moving on. The check should target the actual error.

## Use calculator and non calculator practice appropriately

Check which paper permits a calculator rather than borrowing another board's routine. Practise negative values, fractions and brackets on the device used for the exam. An implausible display should trigger a check of the input and the mathematical model.

Without a calculator, show manageable intermediate steps. For example, finding three eighths of 120 can begin with 120 ÷ 8 = 15, followed by 15 × 3 = 45. The written steps make it easier to check which operation went wrong.

## Rehearse moving on without giving up

A difficult question should not consume the time needed for several accessible ones. Practise leaving clear working, marking the question for return and continuing. Return within the paper when time permits, using the question's information rather than an anxious guess at a familiar formula.

Between papers, avoid assuming a topic is finished because it appeared earlier. Review recurring weaknesses and maintain reliable methods. Protect sleep, food and travel arrangements. Those practical decisions belong in the preparation plan too.

## Know what a final revision lesson should achieve

A useful session should leave the student able to do something independently that was previously unclear. That might be distinguishing direct from reverse percentages, forming an equation from a diagram, or managing a short mixed section. It should not depend on a promise to predict the paper.
