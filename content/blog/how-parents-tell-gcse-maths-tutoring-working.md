GCSE Maths tutoring is easier to review when you know what it is meant to change. A completed worksheet, a cheerful lesson and a higher practice score can all be useful information, but none alone shows the whole picture. Look at what the student can now do without help.

Agree an initial learning priority with the tutor and keep a sample of independent work. That gives you something concrete to revisit. The aim is not to test the child every evening; it is to understand whether the teaching is becoming usable knowledge.

## Choose a goal you can actually observe

“Improve Maths” is too broad for a useful early review. A clearer goal might be forming an equation from a word problem, solving it accurately and checking the answer. Ask what starting evidence led to that priority and how it connects with the student's school course.

For a learner working near a pass, the goal might involve ratios or percentages. For another student, it might involve a more demanding algebraic argument. The target should follow the current work and confirmed tier rather than a generic promise of rapid grade improvement.

## Compare a new question with an earlier attempt

Here is an original example: a number is multiplied by 5 and then 7 is added, giving 42. The equation is 5x + 7 = 42, so x = 7. An early attempt might subtract 7 correctly but stop at 35 without dividing by 5.

After teaching, try a changed version: four times a number plus 9 equals 37. Can the learner write 4x + 9 = 37, obtain x = 7 and check it? The repeated answer happens to be 7, but the evidence lies in the reasoning and working, not recognising a familiar number.

Later, use a question with different structure or mix equations with another topic. The learner must recognise which method applies. If the tutor still needs to name each step, the idea may require further teaching even when the final answers are correct.

## Look for retention as well as immediate success

Understanding during a lesson is encouraging. Being able to return to the idea after a gap provides stronger evidence that it has become available independently. Ask how the tutor revisits earlier priorities alongside new work.

A useful review might show that the student now forms equations reliably but makes occasional negative number errors. That is a more informative update than “doing well.” It tells you what has changed and what the next lesson should address. ## Use school results with appropriate context

School assessments offer another view, especially when completed independently. Ask the teacher how the student is progressing in the current course. A tutor's examples and school evidence can complement each other; they do not need to produce an identical number every week.

Compare like with like where possible. A short topic quiz is not the same as a mixed paper under time pressure. [Official grading guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/gcse-and-a-level-grading-what-you-need-to-know/gcse-and-a-level-grading-what-you-need-to-know) explains that boundaries vary with assessment difficulty, so an isolated percentage should not be presented as a guaranteed future grade.

## Ask the student about the learning itself

Try “Which part makes more sense now?” or “Can you show me how you would start?” instead of only asking whether they like the tutor. A comfortable relationship helps students admit confusion, while a specific explanation shows what they have understood.

Notice changes such as attempting questions previously left blank, explaining a correction or checking a result without a reminder. Confidence matters when it supports participation. It should sit alongside learning evidence, rather than become the only measure because the work is difficult to review.

## Discuss limited progress before buying more time

If the same uncertainty remains, ask what will change. The starting topic, explanation, practice or session arrangement may need adjusting.
