An equation looks manageable. A function question using the same numbers suddenly feels unfamiliar. For many ACT students, the difficulty is not an entirely new topic. It is recognising a mathematical relationship when it appears in a different form.

Algebra and functions deserve careful attention because solving, interpreting and modelling are connected skills. ## Read function notation as an instruction

Let f(x) = 3x − 2. Finding f(4) means using 4 as the input, so the output is 10. Finding the input for which f(x) = 4 is a different task: solve 3x − 2 = 4, giving x = 2.

Ask your child to explain that difference aloud. If both prompts lead to substituting 4, the problem is interpretation rather than arithmetic. Practise pairs of questions that ask for the input and output separately.

Then show a table of the same relationship. The student should recognise that the input-output pattern has not changed simply because the equation is no longer the starting point.

## Repair algebra before building longer solutions

A small manipulation error can spoil otherwise sound reasoning. In 2(x + 5) = 18, both terms inside the brackets must be multiplied by 2. In a fraction equation, an operation applied to one side must be applied to the other side too.

Use a short set to find the precise weak point. Negative signs, indices, rearrangement and fractions should not be grouped together as one vague algebra problem. A student confident with expansion may still need help handling division by an expression.

After correcting a step, check it by substitution when appropriate. If x = 4 is proposed for 2(x + 5) = 18, the left side becomes 18. That check ties manipulation back to the original equation.

## Connect a graph to its equation

For y = 2x + 3, the intercept is 3 and the rate of change is 2. Ask what those numbers would mean if y represented a cost and x represented hours. The starting charge and hourly rate play different roles.

Now present two points, such as (1, 5) and (3, 9), and reconstruct the relationship. The slope is the change in y divided by the change in x, giving 4 divided by 2. The student should connect the graph, points and equation instead of learning three disconnected procedures.

Do not assume a drawing is accurately scaled. Use labelled information and the mathematical relationship when making a conclusion.

## Notice restrictions and features

Some expressions are undefined for particular inputs. For f(x) = 1/(x − 2), x = 2 is excluded because the denominator would be zero. Cancelling an expression elsewhere does not automatically remove an original restriction.

A quadratic also has features beyond its roots. Questions may concern an intercept, a maximum or minimum, or what happens when the input changes. ## Test recognition without announcing the topic

Once the learner can work accurately on a focused set, mix equations, graphs and contextual problems. Ask them to choose a method before calculating. Our article on [changing ACT Math practice scores](act-math-practice-scores-changing.html) explains why the pattern of errors can be more useful than one total.

A useful next lesson should follow the evidence. Perhaps the learner needs notation explained, or perhaps they need stronger manipulation before handling functions. In either case, progress means meeting a new question and understanding the relationship well enough to begin independently.
