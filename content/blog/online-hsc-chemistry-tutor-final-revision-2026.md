If your child is looking for an online HSC Chemistry tutor this October, the most useful starting point is a question they attempted without help. A page of highlighted notes shows what they have read. Their working shows whether they can turn chemical knowledge into an answer when the wording changes.

The [official 2026 HSC timetable](https://www.nsw.gov.au/education-and-training/nesa/key-dates/hsc-written-exam-timetable) places the written exam period between 13 October and 5 November. Check the personalised timetable for Chemistry and plan around the student's other subjects. A focused revision session should leave them knowing what to practise next, rather than feeling they must relearn everything.

## Find the step that breaks the calculation

Consider this original example. A student dissolves 2.00 g of sodium hydroxide to make 250 mL of solution. Given a molar mass of 40.0 g per mole, what is the concentration?

First calculate the amount: 2.00 ÷ 40.0 = 0.0500 mol. Convert the volume to 0.250 L. Then concentration is 0.0500 ÷ 0.250 = 0.200 mol per litre. Each line has a purpose. Dividing by 250 without converting the units produces a very different answer.

A tutor should ask why the volume needs converting and what the concentration means. Next, change the mass or volume and let the student work alone. If the method only works while someone is talking them through it, the learning is not secure yet.

## Explain equilibrium with a chain of reasoning

Students sometimes recognise the phrase “shift to the right” but cannot explain the change. For an equilibrium involving gases, start with the balanced equation and count gaseous particles on each side. Then decide whether the proposed change favours one direction.

If pressure increases because the volume decreases at constant temperature, the system favours the side with fewer moles of gas, where there is a difference. If both sides have equal gaseous amounts, that reasoning does not predict a shift. The condition matters as much as the remembered rule.

Ask the student to describe the disturbance, the response and the effect on a named substance. Also distinguish position from speed: a catalyst increases the rates of both forward and reverse reactions without changing the equilibrium position. A useful lesson checks these distinctions through unfamiliar examples.

## Treat practical evidence as part of Chemistry

An experiment question may need more than a definition of reliability. The student should identify what was measured, which variable changed and what the evidence can support. Repeating measurements can reveal variability, but repetition cannot automatically remove a consistent calibration error.

For a titration task, ask why rinsing the burette with the solution it will contain matters, or how overshooting the endpoint affects the recorded titre. Link the procedural choice to its chemical consequence. This makes a practical answer more precise than saying the experiment should simply be “more accurate.”

## Make the remaining revision manageable

Choose two recurring weaknesses from recent work. For each, use a short cycle: attempt a question, identify the exact error, revise the relevant idea and answer a new question later. Keep a brief record of the method or explanation that changed.

Mix corrected topics with material the student already knows. Otherwise, success on a page labelled “concentration” may only show that the heading supplied the method. A mixed set checks whether they can recognise which idea applies without that clue. Protect time for other exams and rest. A longer timetable is not necessarily a better one. What matters is whether the student can complete the next task independently and explain why the approach works.
