A January Edexcel IAL Maths resit should begin with a change in preparation, not just another collection of past papers. The earlier result tells you that something needs attention. It does not tell you whether the main issue was topic knowledge, inaccurate algebra, unfamiliar wording or unfinished questions.

This guide is for students whose resit entry has already been arranged. Hong Kong candidates should check their entered units and exam dates with their centre. The [registration guide](pearson-edexcel-ial-january-2027-hong-kong-registration.html) explains the January series and the deadlines that have already passed.

## Review each unit rather than the overall grade alone

Write down the units taken, their results and the ones entered for January. A single overall grade can hide a strong applied unit and a much weaker pure unit. Revision should match the components being retaken.

Talk to your exams officer about qualification requirements, your UCI and whether a new cash-in is needed. [Pearson's resit guidance](https://support.pearson.com/uk/articles/en_US/Knowledge/Exams-Resit-Rules) identifies a specific exception for the IAL Mathematics suite. Do not apply a general rule from another subject or calculate a final award from a raw paper percentage.

Entry and aggregation questions belong with the centre. A tutor can help improve mathematical performance, but should not replace the official checks needed to ensure that the right results contribute to your qualification.

## Find the recurring mistake behind the lost marks

Use available scripts, recent practice or a fresh diagnostic paper. Keep the student’s working alongside the mark scheme. A question answered incorrectly is a starting point for investigation, not a complete diagnosis.

Consider this original equation: 2/(x − 1) = 3. Multiplying both sides by x − 1 gives 2 = 3(x − 1), then 2 = 3x − 3, so x = 5/3. The value x = 1 is excluded because it makes the original denominator zero.

A learner who writes 2 = 3x − 1 may understand the overall strategy but distribute inaccurately. A learner who cannot choose the first step needs more support with equations containing fractions. Both could lose marks, but the teaching response should differ.

## Make the correction transferable

Ask the student to explain why multiplication removes the denominator and why the excluded value matters. Then use a fresh example such as 4/(x + 2) = 2. Solve it independently and substitute the answer back into the original equation.

Include that question again within a mixed set several days later. Improvement on a nearly identical question immediately after seeing the method is useful practice; success after a delay is better evidence that the correction has lasted.

Keep an error record short enough to use. Include the question type, what went wrong and a specific action. “Check multiplication across the whole bracket” provides a concrete reminder. “Stop making mistakes” does not.

## Plan the workload around the actual resit entry

If several units are being retaken, assign time according to difficulty and progress rather than dividing it equally by habit. Keep schoolwork and other exam subjects in the plan. A resit schedule that removes all rest is unlikely to remain sustainable.

Begin with targeted topic work, move into mixed questions and then practise complete timed papers. Use official materials that match the unit. Check calculator settings and the required formula resources, and rehearse showing enough working for the examiner to follow.

After each paper, choose the next task from the errors it revealed. Repeating entire papers without reviewing why marks were lost can recreate the same score without changing the underlying method.
