If you are looking for an online Year 2 Maths tutor, you may want more than extra worksheets. Perhaps your child counts carefully but loses track when subtracting, or gets a correct answer without being able to explain it. A good starting lesson should identify what they understand about number and build from there.

England's [Year 2 mathematics programme](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/national-curriculum-in-england-mathematics-programmes-of-study/national-curriculum-in-england-mathematics-programmes-of-study) includes addition and subtraction using practical resources, representations and mental methods. Schools can organise teaching differently within the key stage, so share the child's current schoolwork with the tutor.

## Check what subtraction means to your child

Start with eight counters and remove three. Ask how many remain and what changed. Then place eight counters beside a group of five and ask how many more the larger group contains. Both situations involve a difference of three, but they tell different stories.

Some children can handle taking away yet find comparison confusing. This is useful information, not a reason to rush to a written procedure. An online lesson can use objects at home, a clear camera view or a shared visual so the child can show the relationship.

Ask the learner to tell a story for 12 − 4. If their story involves adding four things, revisit the meaning before increasing the numbers. A short conversation can reveal more than a page of unexplained answers.

## Make crossing a ten visible

Consider 32 − 7. On a number line, subtract two to reach 30, then subtract the remaining five to reach **25**. Ask why the seven was split into two and five. The child should connect the split with the distance to the nearest ten.

Counters can show the same idea. Build 32 with three groups of ten and two ones. Exchange one ten for ten ones, giving two tens and 12 ones. Remove seven ones; two tens and five ones remain.

The point is to connect the representations, not require every child to use every method. Ask which explanation helps them see the calculation. Then try 41 − 6 and gradually reduce the prompts. A successful new attempt matters more than copying 32 − 7 perfectly.

## Show that subtraction can also find a gap

For 52 − 49, counting up may be efficient. Move from 49 to 50, then to 52. The jumps are one and two, so the difference is **three**. This approach is especially helpful when the numbers are close together.

Compare that with 52 − 18, where another method may be more comfortable. Avoid telling the child that a single technique is always fastest. Give them opportunities to notice number relationships and explain their choice.

Check using addition. If the gap between 49 and 52 is three, then 49 + 3 should make 52. This connects the operations and gives the learner a way to test an answer without asking an adult immediately.

## Look for participation during an online lesson

The child should move objects, mark a number line, explain a choice or attempt a question. Watching a tutor solve every calculation may feel smooth but gives limited evidence of what the learner can do alone.

Ask how the tutor will keep the screen readable and the pace manageable. A parent might help set up materials at first, while allowing the child space to respond. Agree on one small practice task for afterwards rather than a large worksheet that creates another argument.

## Choose progress you can recognise

Look for your child explaining a subtraction story, choosing a suitable method and checking a fresh answer with less help. Speed can develop alongside those habits; it should not hide uncertainty about what the numbers mean.
