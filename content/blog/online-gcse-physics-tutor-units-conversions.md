An **online GCSE Physics tutor** should check the steps before the calculator as carefully as the final answer. A student may choose an appropriate equation and substitute the printed numbers correctly, yet get the wrong result because the quantities use different units. More equation practice alone may miss that problem.

This is a useful October focus before school assessments: choose a few recent calculation errors and ask where each one began. Separate understanding the situation, converting units, rearranging the equation and calculating. That makes the next lesson more precise than a general request to improve Physics.

## Read the quantities before choosing numbers

Consider a simple example: an object travels 150 centimetres in 0.5 seconds. To express its average speed in metres per second, first convert the distance to 1.5 metres. Dividing 1.5 by 0.5 gives 3 metres per second.

Using 150 directly gives 300 centimetres per second. That is the same speed in another unit, but it does not answer a question asking for metres per second. The issue is not that the calculation is meaningless; it is that the unit must match the requested quantity.

Ask the learner to write each value with its unit before substitution. This simple habit makes mismatches visible and gives the tutor a way to see whether the student understands what each number represents.

## Build conversions from a known relationship

One metre contains one hundred centimetres. A distance expressed in centimetres therefore has a smaller numerical value when expressed in metres. Use a familiar length to check the direction of the conversion instead of relying on a remembered instruction to move a decimal point.

The same reasoning can help with grams and kilograms, or minutes and seconds. State the relationship, decide which unit is needed and check whether the resulting number is sensible. Different quantities require different conversions, so avoid treating every prefix as an identical operation.

Practise conversions separately for a short period, then put them back inside a Physics question. Otherwise the student may complete a conversion worksheet confidently but fail to notice when a conversion is required in context.

## Keep the equation connected to the situation

If the question asks for average speed, identify the distance travelled and the time taken. Ask what division represents here: the distance covered per unit of time. This meaning provides a useful check on the equation and the final unit.

For a rearrangement, let the student work with the symbols before adding the values. Mixing conversion, rearrangement and substitution on one line makes an error harder to locate. Clear working is useful for thinking, not just for presenting a neat answer.

## Confirm the exact GCSE course

AQA separate Physics and Combined Science are different qualifications. Tier and examination year also matter when selecting material. The [AQA GCSE Physics specification](https://www.aqa.org.uk/subjects/physics/gcse/physics-8463/specification) is an authoritative starting point for the separate Physics course; other courses need their own specification.

A tutor should check current official arrangements rather than reuse an old assumption about equation sheets or assessment requirements. Even when an equation is supplied, the learner still needs to identify the quantities, manage units and use the relationship correctly.

Bring the paper or school worksheet as well as the student's answer. Seeing only the final number removes the information needed to distinguish a reading error from an algebra or conversion error.

## Ask for an independent check after teaching

After the worked speed example, change both the distance and time. Ask the learner to explain the required conversion without being prompted. Then give a question whose quantities already use compatible units and see whether they recognise that no conversion is needed.

This avoids teaching a routine where the child automatically divides every number by one hundred. Useful progress means selecting a step because the question requires it, not because the previous example used it.
