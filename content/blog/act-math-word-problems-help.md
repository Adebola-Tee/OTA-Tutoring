A student can solve an equation quickly and still struggle to create it from a paragraph. ACT Math word problems expose that difference. The challenge is often deciding what the numbers represent before any calculation begins.

Try asking your child to explain the situation without using the numbers. If they cannot describe the relationship, a faster calculation will not help. ## Name the unknown in ordinary language

Suppose a delivery service charges a fixed $6 plus $2 for each parcel. A bill totals $24. Let n represent the number of parcels, not the total bill or the charge per parcel. The relationship is 6 + 2n = 24, so n = 9.

Before solving, ask which amount stays fixed and which changes. That separates the setup from the manipulation. If a student writes 6n + 2 = 24, return to the meaning of the charges rather than treating the equation as a careless slip.

A short note beside the variable is often enough to keep the target clear throughout the solution.

## Track what each unit describes

Consider a car travelling 150 kilometres in 2.5 hours. Dividing distance by time gives an average speed of 60 kilometres per hour. Multiplying produces a quantity that does not answer the question.

Units can help identify the required operation. They also reveal when minutes must be converted to hours or centimetres to metres. Do that conversion explicitly instead of hoping the calculator expression will compensate for inconsistent measurements.

Practise related questions that ask for distance, speed and time separately. The student should understand one relationship from different directions, not memorise three unrelated recipes.

## Be careful with comparison language

If Amir has three times as many tokens as Bea, the relationship is A = 3B. The order of names matters. Assigning a small example, such as Bea having 4 and Amir having 12, can check whether an equation makes sense.

Percentages create similar traps. A $60 item reduced by 25% costs $45. A later 25% increase acts on $45, giving $56.25, not the original $60. The comparison base changed.

Encourage your child to identify what each percentage is a percentage of. Hunting only for a keyword such as increase or total can lead to the wrong operation when the situation is more complicated.

## Break a long prompt into relationships

A question may contain several sentences but only two useful relationships. Write each separately, then combine them. For a mixture or ticket problem, one equation might describe a total number and another a total cost.

Do not discard information simply because it appears in a longer sentence. Instead, decide whether it changes the model or only supplies context. Drawing a small table can help keep categories and units organised.

The [official ACT Math description](https://www.act.org/content/act/en/products-and-services/the-act/test-preparation/description-of-math-test.html) includes modelling and combining skills across topics. Being comfortable with school arithmetic is therefore a useful starting point, but it does not replace practice constructing relationships.

## Check the answer against the story

Substitute the result back into the original situation. Nine parcels at $2 each cost $18; adding the fixed $6 gives $24. The proposed answer satisfies both the arithmetic and the story.

Check whether the answer should be a whole number, a positive length or a plausible probability. An impossible result is a cue to inspect the setup before choosing an option.
