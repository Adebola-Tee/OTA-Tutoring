A parent searching for an online Grade 3 English tutor in Canada may want help with reading, writing or both. Before choosing a lesson package, find out what your child can already do independently. A short story discussion and a few written sentences are often enough to make the first conversation more useful.

Bring the province, school expectations and a recent assignment. Canada does not have one national school curriculum. For example, British Columbia's [Grade 3 English Language Arts curriculum](https://www.curriculum.gov.bc.ca/curriculum/english-language-arts/3/core) connects reading, oral communication and writing. That is a helpful reference for BC families, rather than a substitute for another province's requirements.

## Find where the reading becomes difficult

Listen to a short passage and ask the child to explain what happened. Notice whether unfamiliar words interrupt the reading, whether sentences sound fluent and whether the retelling includes the main events. Different observations suggest different lesson priorities.

If reading individual words is difficult, ask how the tutor will support decoding and practise it in suitable texts. If the child reads smoothly but cannot explain the story, the work may need to focus on vocabulary, connections between sentences or finding evidence.

Avoid judging understanding from reading speed alone. A child who pauses to correct a word or reread a confusing line may be making a useful decision. Ask what caused the pause and whether the meaning became clearer afterwards.

## Try a short reading task before choosing support

Use our original example: “Lena held the library book under her coat as she ran through the rain. At the door, she checked the pages before taking off her wet shoes.” Ask why Lena put the book under her coat.

A useful answer is that she wanted to keep it dry. Ask which details support that idea. The rain and her checking the pages make that explanation reasonable. “Because she was cold” is possible in another story, but this passage gives stronger evidence about protecting the book.

Then ask the child to retell the event in their own words. Can they connect the action with its purpose? Finally, ask them to write one answer sentence. This shows how reading understanding, oral explanation and writing fit together.

## Help the child write an idea they can explain

A child may give a clear spoken answer and then write only “rain.” Start by rehearsing a complete sentence aloud: “Lena covered the book because she wanted to keep the pages dry.” Let the learner write their own version, then read it back.

Check whether the sentence says what they intended. Choose one editing focus, such as a capital letter and a full stop, before trying to correct every error. When the idea is clear, extend the task with a sentence about what Lena might do next.

Keep the task short enough to finish and discuss. A tutor rewriting the whole piece can produce an attractive result while leaving the learner unsure how it was improved. The child should be able to describe one change and use it again.

## Ask how online lessons will hold their attention

Look for short sections with a clear purpose: reading together, talking about meaning, writing and checking. Shared screens should be readable, and the learner needs opportunities to answer rather than watch a long presentation.

Ask what materials you should prepare and how much help a parent will need to provide. Agree how the tutor will communicate a small practice target after each lesson. A few purposeful minutes between sessions can help a child retain a method.

Compare independent work after several lessons with the starting sample. Is the child locating evidence with fewer prompts? Are written answers more complete?
