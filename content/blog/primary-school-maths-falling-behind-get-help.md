Should we give it another term? That is a reasonable question when a primary school child finds maths difficult. Children do not all become confident at the same speed, and one challenging lesson does not require a tutoring package.

Waiting becomes less helpful when the same gap keeps blocking new learning. Early support can begin with a conversation and a small teaching adjustment, rather than a dramatic claim that your child is falling permanently behind.

## Ask the school for a precise starting point

Instead of asking only whether your child is doing well, ask which skill currently needs most attention. Can they count objects reliably, understand tens and ones, compare quantities or explain equal groups?

Request one example of a task they can complete independently and one that needs help. This gives you a clearer picture than a broad label such as below expected level. It also helps a future tutor connect lessons to classroom teaching.

If the child recently changed schools, languages or curricula, mention that context. A method or term may be unfamiliar even when the underlying idea is partly understood. Support should take account of that transition.

## Try a short everyday example

For a child learning place value, make 34 with three groups of ten and four ones. Ask what changes if one more ten is added. The quantity becomes 44, not 35. Let the child explain rather than simply watching your demonstration.

For subtraction, ask how many more objects are needed to go from seven to ten. Compare this with taking seven away from ten. Both relate to the same number fact, but they represent different situations.

Keep the activity brief and suitable for the school level. You are looking for understanding, not creating a surprise test at the kitchen table. Stop if frustration is taking over and share the observation with the teacher.

## Decide whether the difficulty is recurring

Look across several weeks of ordinary work. If your child repeatedly cannot start tasks that depend on an earlier skill, or needs extensive adult help to finish, ask what additional teaching is available.

A persistent difficulty with place value can affect addition, subtraction and later decimals. That is a reason to address the foundation rather than waiting until the worksheet becomes longer.

The [government's primary maths teaching guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/teaching-mathematics-in-primary-schools) provides a useful reference for UK learners. It should support discussion, not become a checklist used to compare your child with every other child. Other curricula may introduce ideas in a different order.

## Choose support the child can participate in

Primary Maths tutoring should involve the learner showing, explaining and trying. Objects, pictures and written symbols can be connected within the same lesson. A child should not spend the whole session passively watching someone complete questions.

Our guide to [fractions foundations](child-confused-by-fractions-foundations.html) shows how a teacher can investigate understanding before introducing a calculation rule. The same principle applies to early number work.

Online lessons need a practical setup too. The tutor should see the child's working, and the child should be able to hear instructions clearly. An adult may help with equipment initially without answering questions on the learner's behalf.
