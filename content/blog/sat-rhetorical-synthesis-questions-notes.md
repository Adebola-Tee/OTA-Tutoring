SAT rhetorical synthesis questions can look reassuring: the information is supplied as notes. Yet students often choose an answer that is factually correct and misses the task. The essential decision is which sentence uses the notes to achieve the stated purpose.

If you are preparing for 7 November 2026, College Board lists the regular registration deadline as 23 October on its [registration page](https://satsuite.collegeboard.org/sat/registration). Check the current arrangements for your test centre. A revision plan should develop the skills your work shows you need, rather than treating the deadline itself as a reason to practise everything equally.

## Start with what the question wants

College Board places rhetorical synthesis within Expression of Ideas in its [Reading and Writing specifications](https://satsuite.collegeboard.org/k12-educators/about/alignment/reading). The task involves selecting and presenting information for a particular rhetorical goal.

Translate the instruction into a short action: show a similarity, explain a difference, introduce a subject or emphasise a particular result. Keep that action in view while reading the choices. A sentence can use accurate facts from the notes without completing the requested action.

You do not need prior expertise in the subject of the notes. Use the information supplied and avoid adding claims that are not supported there.

## Try a comparison using original notes

Suppose an invented practice set says that Lake Arin covers 12 square kilometres and has an average depth of 8 metres. Lake Bel covers 20 square kilometres and also has an average depth of 8 metres. Both lakes support local fishing.

The goal is to emphasise a similarity in average depth. A suitable sentence is “Although their surface areas differ, Lake Arin and Lake Bel both have an average depth of 8 metres.” It makes the requested similarity explicit.

“Lake Bel covers a larger area than Lake Arin” is true according to the notes, but it answers a different goal. A sentence about local fishing would describe a similarity too, but not the specified similarity in depth.

This is an original teaching example, not an official SAT item. Its purpose is to show why matching keywords or selecting any true statement is insufficient.

## Change the goal and test the same information

Now ask for a difference in surface area. The larger area sentence becomes relevant, while a depth comparison no longer completes the task. Nothing about the notes has changed; the required use of those notes has.

Ask yourself which facts are necessary for this goal and which are background. You need not include every detail. An answer that packs in all the notes may be less effective than one that clearly makes the requested relationship.

Check the strength of the claim as well. Shared depths do not establish identical ecosystems, and larger surface area alone does not prove a lake contains more fish. Those statements would add unsupported reasoning.

## Reject answers for a specific reason

When reviewing practice, label a rejected choice precisely. It may be inaccurate, irrelevant to the goal, incomplete or stronger than the evidence permits. “It sounded wrong” gives you little to use next time.

For the chosen answer, explain which words perform the requested job. If the instruction asks for a contrast, identify the contrasting information. If it asks to introduce a person to an unfamiliar audience, check whether the answer actually supplies the relevant identifying context.

This habit also helps with [SAT transition questions](digital-sat-transitions-wrong-connecting-word.html), where the logical relationship between ideas matters more than recognising a familiar connecting word.

## Practise the decision before adding time pressure

Use a small set from official practice resources. First work without rushing and explain your decisions. Then attempt a fresh set under a manageable time limit. Review whether errors come from misreading the goal, overlooking a note or accepting an unsupported statement.

Keep other Reading and Writing skills in your plan. Rhetorical synthesis is one part of the section, and progress on these questions does not guarantee a particular overall score. Recheck the skill with unfamiliar notes rather than repeating answers you remember.
