The answer matches the calculator or the back of the worksheet, but the score is disappointing. For an AP Calculus student, that can feel unfair. Usually the missing information is in the task: the question asked for evidence, interpretation or a mathematical setup as well as a result.

Free response scoring is question-specific. A correct final number may earn a point for that result, but it cannot always supply the other mathematical work the scoring guidelines require.

## Make the method visible

Suppose a rate r(t) describes water entering a tank. A question asks for the amount entering between two times. The student may calculate the correct value with technology but show only the decimal.

Writing the definite integral with the correct function and bounds makes the setup visible. It shows what has been accumulated and over which interval. If the task asks for the amount in the tank, the starting amount may also need to be included.

The learner should understand why each line is present. Adding pages of unexplained calculator output is unlikely to clarify the reasoning.

## Replace vague conclusions with evidence

An answer such as “there is a maximum because the derivative is zero” is incomplete in general. A zero derivative identifies a candidate; it does not automatically establish the requested behaviour.

If the first derivative changes from positive to negative, stating that change and connecting it to increasing then decreasing provides a justification. For an absolute maximum on a closed interval, relevant candidates and endpoints must be considered as appropriate.

This is a common place where a student knows the right answer but has not written enough to demonstrate why it is right.

## Keep quantities and units consistent

If a function measures position in metres and time in seconds, its derivative measures velocity in metres per second. An interpretation should connect the value to the actual quantity and time in the question.

A learner might write “the answer is 6” when the question asks for an interpretation. A fuller statement identifies what is changing, how quickly and when. Where signs matter, distinguish a negative rate from a negative amount.

Precision is especially useful when a question contains several related functions. Name the function, derivative or accumulated quantity rather than repeatedly saying “it”.

## Use scoring guidelines as feedback

Choose a released FRQ and the associated [official AP Calculus AB scoring resources](https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/courses/ap-calculus-ab/exam/past-exam-questions). Compare the student’s answer with the specific mathematical evidence required for each part.

The purpose is to understand the scoring, not to memorise a model response for every possible question. There can be more than one acceptable method. The learner should see which part of their original solution demonstrated understanding and which part was absent or unclear.

Have them repair the answer in their own words. Then use another question that requires the same kind of evidence. This is a better test than reproducing the response they have just read.

## Practise concise explanations

Useful answers are often short. One correct setup, one relevant calculation and one precise conclusion can establish the reasoning. The exact requirements depend on the question, so there is no universal three-line template.

Our article on [why differentiation alone is not enough for FRQs](https://otalearningstudio.com/blog/ap-calculus-frq-beyond-differentiation.html) explains how method selection and interpretation fit into that process.

During practice, ask the student to read the question again before moving on. Have they supplied the requested justification, estimate or interpretation, or only the calculation?
