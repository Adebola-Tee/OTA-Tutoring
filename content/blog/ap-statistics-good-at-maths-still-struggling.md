Your child has always been good at maths. They rearrange equations quickly and usually find the right numerical answer. Then AP Statistics arrives, and responses that look correct to them receive disappointing feedback.

The difficulty may be a change in what counts as a complete answer. Algebra often starts with a clearly defined mathematical relationship. Statistics also asks whether the data are suitable, which assumptions apply and how much the evidence allows us to say. A correct calculation sits inside that larger argument.

## A number does not decide what can be claimed

Imagine a survey finds that students who attend an optional study club have higher average test scores. It is tempting to say that the club caused the improvement. But perhaps students who joined were already more motivated or had higher previous scores.

That is an observational association. Without a suitable experimental design, the difference does not by itself establish cause and effect. A strong calculator user can still miss this point if they focus only on comparing the averages.

Ask your child to describe how the participants were chosen and what else could explain the pattern. These questions are mathematical reasoning about evidence, even though they do not involve solving an equation.

## Learn to work with uncertainty

Some students find statistics frustrating because conclusions are qualified. A sample estimate is not necessarily the exact population value. Different samples can give different results even when the sampling method is sound.

Suppose a random sample suggests that 60% of a school’s students favour a later start time. The estimate describes what the sample indicates about the population; it is not proof that exactly 60% of every student agrees. Sample size and variability affect the precision of an estimate.

Have the learner explain the uncertainty in plain language before introducing more notation. The notation should make an understood idea precise, rather than hiding an unfamiliar idea behind symbols.

## Read the context before applying a rule

When a learner sees a familiar formula, they may start substituting immediately. In statistics, first identify what kind of variable is involved, which population matters and what procedure the question actually calls for.

A proportion concerns a category, such as the fraction of students who cycle to school. A mean concerns a quantitative measurement, such as journey time. Mixing the two is not a small arithmetic error; it changes the analysis.

Use paired questions about the same setting. One asks about cycling; the other asks about travel minutes. Discuss why the appropriate summaries and methods differ despite the shared story.

## Make explanations specific

“The data are reliable” is vague. “The sample was randomly selected from the school register” supplies relevant evidence, although the learner must still consider how the study was carried out. Likewise, a conclusion should identify the population and measured variable.

The [College Board AP Statistics course overview](https://apstudents.collegeboard.org/courses/ap-statistics) includes formulating questions, collecting data, analysing data and interpreting results. Calculation matters, but it cannot replace those stages.

Practice should therefore include short written explanations. Ask the learner to justify a method, interpret a result and identify a limitation. Mark those separately from the arithmetic so the next teaching priority is clear.
