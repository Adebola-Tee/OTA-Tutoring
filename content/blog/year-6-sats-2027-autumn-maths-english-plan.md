A Year 6 child does not need to spend the whole autumn taking SATs papers. They need secure skills that will serve them in class, in the tests and when secondary school begins. If you are considering an **online Year 6 SATs tutor**, October can be used to establish those foundations before revision becomes more urgent.

The [government's updated 2027 information for parents](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/key-stage-2-national-curriculum-tests-and-results-information-for-parents/2027-key-stage-2-national-curriculum-tests-information-for-parents) gives the key stage 2 test period as 10 to 13 May 2027. This guidance concerns England's national curriculum tests. Families elsewhere in the UK should check their own school's assessment arrangements.

## Find the starting point without a full mock

Ask the class teacher which skills would make the biggest difference this term. Bring a recent piece of Maths and English work to a tutor, including the child's working and the feedback. The interesting information is often in the method, not the score.

For Maths, check a small selection: place value, a calculation, a fraction question and a word problem. For English, use a short passage with one retrieval question and one inference question, then ask for a few sentences of writing.

The goal is to distinguish what the learner can do alone from what they can do with support. This helps avoid spending lessons on skills they already have or jumping too quickly into material they cannot yet access.

## Give arithmetic one clear weekly target

If fractions are uncertain, begin with the meaning of the numerator and denominator and the idea of equal parts. Use a diagram, connect it to the written fraction, then try a calculation. Moving straight to a rule can hide a weak understanding.

For example, ask whether three quarters is larger than one half and why. A child who can draw or explain the comparison has a starting point for later calculation. A child who guesses needs more work on the quantities before another worksheet.

Choose a few questions that vary one feature at a time. After guided practice, include a new question without a prompt. Return to the skill the following week so that progress means remembering, not just performing well immediately after teaching.

## Build reading through evidence

Reading practice should include discussion. After a short passage, ask what happened, which detail supports the answer and how a character might feel. Keep inference tied to the wording rather than encouraging imaginative guesses.

A useful answer combines a point with relevant evidence and an explanation. The exact length should suit the question. Longer writing is not automatically better if it does not answer what was asked.

Choose texts the learner can read with enough fluency to understand. If decoding is taking all their attention, talk to the teacher about suitable reading support. Comprehension questions alone will not repair every reading difficulty.

## Teach grammar inside real sentences

Instead of learning only labels, ask children to make and improve sentences. Where does one complete idea end? What does the punctuation make clear? Can a pronoun be replaced when its meaning is ambiguous?

Use a sentence from their own writing, discuss one improvement and ask them to apply it to another sentence. This connects grammar practice to communication and gives the child a reason to care about accuracy.

A manageable week might include a short arithmetic session, shared reading and a writing task. The exact amount should fit school homework, concentration and family commitments. Leave space for reading for pleasure.

## What progress should look like by the end of autumn

Look for fewer prompts, clearer explanations and successful attempts at questions with different wording. A tutor should be able to show one skill that has improved and identify the next target. Constant testing without changes to teaching gives parents little useful information.
