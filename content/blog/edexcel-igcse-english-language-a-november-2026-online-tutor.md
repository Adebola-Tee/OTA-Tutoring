If your child is taking Edexcel International GCSE English Language A this November, “more English practice” is too broad a plan. A learner may need to make reading answers more precise, control the form of transactional writing or develop an imaginative piece. The right online tutor should know which entry and paper are involved before choosing a task.

Pearson's [final November 2026 timetable](https://qualifications.pearson.com/content/dam/pdf/Support/Examination-timetables-for-Edexcel-International-GCSE/intgcse-nov-2026-final.pdf) lists English Language A Paper 1 on 3 November and Paper 2 on 5 November for the linear route, alongside modular units on those dates. Check the exact codes on your entry with your school or centre. English Language B, English as a Second Language and UK GCSE English are different entries.

## Bring the question you actually attempted

Ask the student to bring a marked piece of writing and one reading response, including the question and any feedback. “I always lose marks in English” does not show whether the issue is understanding the source, selecting evidence, organising ideas or finishing in time.

For Paper 1, the published title is “Non-fiction Texts and Transactional Writing.” Paper 2 is “Poetry and Prose Texts and Imaginative Writing.” Those names are a useful reminder that a single generic essay template will not solve both papers. Match each practice session to the paper and route the student is entered for.

## Teach a reading answer to do more than quote

Consider an original teaching extract: “The station clock blinked through the rain while the last bus pulled away.” If a question asks how the writer creates a sense of urgency, simply copying “last bus” is incomplete. The student could explain how “last” suggests a closing opportunity and how the clock draws attention to time running out.

Then ask them to find another detail in a new extract and explain its effect in the context of the question. A tutor should check whether the learner can make that link independently. Technique labels are helpful only when they lead to an explanation of meaning, not when they are listed for decoration.

If a student reads slowly, practise identifying the exact question demand before selecting evidence. If they select too much text, ask which small phrase does the work and why. These are teachable decisions. The solution is more precise feedback, not merely completing more pages.

## Practise the writing task as a real piece of communication

For an original example, imagine a task asking for an email to a school leader proposing a quieter study space. The writer needs a clear audience, a reason for the proposal and practical details. “Dear Sir or Madam, I am writing to inform you” cannot carry the whole response.

Have the student plan two points: noisy study areas make concentration difficult, and a supervised quiet room at lunch could help. Then write an opening that states the request plainly. Review whether the next paragraph supports it with a believable example. The choice of form and register should fit the actual task, not a memorised opening used everywhere.

For imaginative writing, ask what changes between the beginning and end of a scene. A polished description with no movement may leave the story static. Give feedback on one paragraph, let the learner rewrite it and compare the effect. The student's own choices remain central.

## Use the remaining weeks deliberately

Split practice between the entered papers. Alternate a short timed section with careful review, then attempt a fresh task. Track one recurring error at a time, such as unsupported claims, unclear paragraph purpose or an ending rushed by poor timing.
