If your Year 11 child wants an online GCSE Higher Maths tutor before the mocks, ask them to bring an unfinished question as well as one they got wrong. An unfinished attempt can reveal a problem with recognising the method. A wrong answer with sensible working may reveal a smaller calculation slip. Both matter, but they need different teaching.

Mock dates and topic coverage are set by schools, so check the actual schedule rather than assume every student sits the same paper in November. Confirm the board and tier too. [AQA's GCSE Maths specification](https://www.aqa.org.uk/subjects/mathematics/gcse/mathematics-8300/specification/specification-at-a-glance) distinguishes Foundation grades 1 to 5 from Higher grades 4 to 9. Resources should match the student's entry.

## Check algebra before adding harder questions

Higher questions often combine several ideas. A weak basic step can stop a student even when they understand the larger topic. Start by checking substitution, negative numbers, expanding brackets and rearranging equations through a few short independent attempts.

For example, solve 3(2x − 5) = 4x + 9. Expanding gives 6x − 15 = 4x + 9, then 2x = 24 and x = 12. Substituting 12 into the original equation gives 57 on both sides. That final check tests the solution rather than merely repeating the same algebra.

If the student writes 6x − 5, the issue is distributing multiplication to both terms. If they expand correctly but move terms incorrectly, focus on preserving equality. The tutor should locate the first wrong step before introducing a more complicated equation.

## Make unfamiliar problems less mysterious

Consider a rectangle with sides x + 2 and x + 5 and area 40 square units. The student needs to form (x + 2)(x + 5) = 40, expand and obtain x² + 7x − 30 = 0. Factorising gives (x + 10)(x − 3) = 0.

The solutions are x = −10 and x = 3, but only x = 3 gives positive side lengths. The rectangle is therefore 5 by 8. This example checks modelling, expansion, solving and interpreting the result. A tutor can then change the numbers or the shape and ask the learner to choose an approach independently.

## Show working that another person can follow

A student who performs several steps mentally may find it difficult to recover after a slip. Encourage a clear equation, a substitution line where relevant and an answer with its units. The purpose is to make the reasoning visible, not to write a paragraph for every calculation.

For geometry, mark the diagram and state which relationship applies. For probability, define the event before calculating. In a multi-part question, check whether earlier answers are needed. These small habits make practice easier to review and reduce the chance of abandoning useful working.

## Use papers to diagnose rather than guess the mock

Begin with mixed questions that include secure topics and current weaknesses. Record whether each error came from knowledge, method choice, arithmetic or reading. Review the pattern after several attempts rather than creating a new revision plan after every disappointing score.

School feedback should guide the choice of topics, but no tutor should promise to know the exact mock questions. Our article on [understanding Maths in class but struggling with exam questions](understands-maths-class-cannot-answer-gcse-exam-questions.html) explores why recognition during a lesson can differ from independent performance.

## Make the next fortnight specific

Choose two priorities and practise them in short sessions between lessons. Revisit corrected questions after a gap, then use a new version to check whether the method transfers. Add timed work once the learner can explain the approach reliably.

Ask the tutor to show what has become independent and what still needs prompting. Progress may first appear as fewer abandoned questions or clearer working, before it appears as a larger overall score.

## Start with your child's actual work

Bring the school's mock schedule, board, tier and a recent marked attempt. Share the target grade and the questions that feel hardest to start. That makes the first conversation useful.
