When ACT Science became optional, many parents of future engineers, doctors and scientists assumed their child must still take it. Others assumed “optional” meant it could be ignored.

Both reactions are too broad. A STEM interest makes the decision more relevant, but it does not replace checking the student’s actual destinations and testing profile.

## Optional does not mean every institution treats it identically

ACT’s current [test information](https://www.act.org/content/act/en/products-and-services/the-act.html) says Science is optional and does not affect the Composite score. However, universities, scholarships and specialist programmes make their own decisions about what they require, recommend or consider.

Build a list of realistic targets and read each official admissions page. If the wording is unclear, contact the institution directly. Do not base the choice on a social-media summary that may be outdated.

## Ask whether the score adds useful evidence

For some STEM applicants, a strong Science score may complement rigorous coursework and show confidence with scientific data. For others, transcripts, AP or IB results and other parts of the application may already provide substantial evidence.

The question is not whether Science “looks good” in the abstract. It is whether the score adds value for this student at these destinations.

## Measure the preparation trade-off

Every additional test section uses time and attention. If ACT Science is already a strength, the preparation cost may be modest. If the student is struggling badly with a core section, adding Science could spread effort too thinly.

Compare three things:

- current timed Science performance
- the work needed to reach a credible target
- what that same time could improve elsewhere

This prevents the STEM label from overriding sensible planning.

## Classroom science success does not guarantee ACT Science success

The section measures scientific reasoning: extracting values, comparing experiments, evaluating claims and interpreting graphs. It does not mainly reward memorising advanced content.

A strong STEM student can still run out of time by reading every passage slowly or doing unnecessary calculations. Conversely, a student with gaps in school science may score well by using the information provided accurately.

## Use a current-format diagnostic

ACT currently describes Science as a 40-minute optional section with 40 questions, 34 of which are scored. Use recent official material that matches that structure.

After the diagnostic, classify errors:

1. data interpretation
2. scientific investigation
3. evaluating models and evidence
4. timing and passage navigation
5. limited background knowledge

This shows whether improvement requires reasoning practice, pacing work or selective content support.

## A sensible decision rule

Take the section when a target requires or meaningfully values it, or when a strong score can be achieved without harming higher priorities. Consider skipping when it has no clear application value and preparation would weaken the core ACT or school performance.

For a broader family checklist, see [whether your child should take optional ACT Science in 2026–2027](https://otalearningstudio.com/blog/optional-act-science-2026-2027-should-child-take-it.html).

Parents can help by separating identity from strategy. A student can be committed to STEM whether or not they take one optional section. The decision should be evidence-led and destination-specific.

If the student does take Science, preparation should support—not compete with—their wider academic story. Set a target based on realistic practice evidence, use a defined weekly time allowance and review progress after several focused sessions. A strong plan has a stopping rule: if improvement requires disproportionate time, reconsider the balance rather than continuing from pride or fear.
