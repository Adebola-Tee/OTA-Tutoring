Your child remembers learning circle area. Put a circle inside a rectangle, though, and they cannot see where to start. What looks like forgotten geometry may be difficulty recognising the shape, choosing the relevant measurement or combining two familiar ideas.

Before asking for more memorisation, inspect one original solution. ACT geometry becomes easier to organise when the student links the diagram to a relationship and explains why that relationship applies. That is the focus of useful revision and of targeted online ACT Maths lessons.

## Check the measurements before the formula

Give a circle with diameter 12. Ask the learner to label its radius before calculating area. The radius is 6, so the area is 36π. Writing 144π would suggest that the diameter was used in place of the radius, not necessarily that the area formula was forgotten.

Ask for the units as well. An area is measured in square units, whereas circumference is a length. A number without the right type of measurement may conceal confusion about what the question requests.

Make this check part of practice. The student should identify the target quantity, mark the relevant measurements and only then select a formula.

## Rebuild a relationship with a drawing

A formula is easier to use when it describes something the learner understands. For the area of a triangle, draw a rectangle and divide it along a diagonal. Two congruent triangles share the rectangle's area, explaining the factor of one half.

The height must be perpendicular to the chosen base. A sloping side is not automatically the height. Rotate the drawing and ask the student to identify the same base-height pair. This checks whether understanding survives a less familiar orientation.

For a right triangle with legs 5 and 12, the hypotenuse is 13. Ask why the right angle matters before using Pythagoras. Applying the relationship to any triangle is a different mistake from remembering it incorrectly.

## Distinguish lengths from areas and volumes

If two similar shapes have corresponding lengths in the ratio 2 to 3, their areas are in the ratio 4 to 9. Simply multiplying an area by 3/2 misses the fact that both dimensions change.

Use rectangles to make that visible. A 2 by 4 rectangle has area 8; a similar 3 by 6 rectangle has area 18. Comparing 8 and 18 gives 4 to 9. The numbers explain the scaling rule instead of leaving it as another isolated fact.

For similar solids, volume scales with the cube of the length factor. Do not introduce all three rules at once if the student is still uncertain about area.

## Space out recall and mix the diagrams

Review a small group of relationships, then revisit them on another day without the formula list. Include a rotated triangle, a composite area and a diagram containing an unnecessary measurement. Recognition needs practice too.

Use [ACT's official mathematics description](https://www.act.org/content/act/en/products-and-services/the-act/test-preparation/description-of-math-test.html) to check relevant geometry coverage. Avoid assuming that every diagram should be solved by appearance; read stated conditions and labels carefully.

A short written explanation can reveal more than ten copied formulas. Ask what information is needed and what could be ignored. Our guide to [ACT Math pacing](act-math-running-out-of-time.html) also explains why a clear setup can save time later.
