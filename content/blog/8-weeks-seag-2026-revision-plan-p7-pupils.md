Eight weeks is enough time to make a real difference before the **SEAG Transfer Test 2026** — provided your child is revising the right things.

The aim should not be to cram every topic every evening.

A better approach is to spend the first few weeks fixing weaknesses, then gradually move towards **mixed questions, timed practice and full SEAG papers**.

With **Paper 1 taking place on Saturday 14 November 2026 and Paper 2 on Saturday 21 November 2026**, here is a practical eight-week revision plan P7 families can follow.

## Week 1: Find the gaps

Before increasing revision, find out where marks are actually being lost.

Give your child a mixture of English and Maths questions and note the patterns.

For English, check:

* comprehension
* vocabulary
* grammar
* spelling
* punctuation

For Maths, look at:

* number skills
* fractions, decimals and percentages
* problem-solving
* measurement
* shape and space
* money
* data

Do not simply record the score.

Write down **why each incorrect answer was wrong**.

That mistake list becomes your revision plan.

## Week 2: Strengthen core Maths skills

This week should focus heavily on accuracy.

Revise weaker number skills first, particularly calculations your child should be able to complete confidently without becoming stuck.

Then introduce short word problems.

A useful rule is:

**calculate less blindly and think more carefully.**

Before solving a Maths problem, your child should ask:

1. What information have I been given?
2. What am I being asked to find?
3. Which operation or method do I need?

This small habit can prevent many avoidable errors.

## Week 3: Build stronger English comprehension

For many pupils, comprehension is not difficult because they cannot read.

It becomes difficult because they **read too quickly**.

Practise short passages followed by questions on:

* retrieving information
* inference
* vocabulary in context
* the meaning of phrases
* identifying evidence from the text

Encourage your child to return to the passage rather than answering from memory.

The correct answer should come from what the text actually says — not what sounds most likely.

## Week 4: Mix English and Maths

By this point, revision should begin looking more like the real SEAG assessment.

Both SEAG papers include **English and Maths within the same assessment**, so pupils need to become comfortable moving between different types of thinking.

Try shorter mixed sessions such as:

**20 minutes English + 20 minutes Maths + 10 minutes correcting mistakes.**

Keep a notebook of recurring errors.

If the same type of question keeps appearing, that is no longer a careless mistake.

It is a topic that needs attention.

## Week 5: Start serious timed practice

Now introduce more time pressure.

SEAG Paper 1 and Paper 2 each contain:

* 28 English questions
* 28 Maths questions
* 60 minutes for the main assessment

Your child therefore cannot afford to spend several minutes fighting with one difficult question.

Practise three habits:

* answer straightforward questions efficiently
* do not panic when one question feels difficult
* leave enough time to check the answer sheet

Timing should improve gradually.

The goal is not to make your child rush.

The goal is to make good decisions under time pressure.

## Week 6: Complete a full practice paper

This is where preparation becomes more realistic.

Use an official SEAG practice paper and recreate test conditions as closely as possible.

No interruptions.

No help midway through.

No stopping the clock because a question is difficult.

Afterwards, spend as much time reviewing the paper as you spent completing it.

Divide mistakes into three groups:

**Knowledge gap:** the topic was not understood.

**Technique error:** your child knew the topic but approached the question badly.

**Careless error:** the correct method was known but accuracy was lost.

That distinction tells you what the final weeks should focus on.

## Week 7: Target weak areas — not everything

This is not the week to restart the entire curriculum.

Look at the previous six weeks and identify the **three biggest weaknesses**.

Perhaps your child is losing marks through:

* inference questions
* vocabulary
* fractions
* multi-step problems
* punctuation
* rushing

Spend most revision time repairing those weaknesses while still keeping some mixed practice.

At this stage, targeted preparation is much more valuable than completing worksheet after worksheet.

## Week 8: Build confidence and sharpen exam technique

The final week should not feel like punishment.

Avoid suddenly doubling revision hours.

Instead, use shorter focused sessions to keep skills sharp.

Practise:

* reading every question properly
* eliminating obviously wrong answers
* moving on when a question is taking too long
* checking calculations
* transferring answers carefully
* staying calm after a difficult question

SEAG includes two assessment papers, so children should also understand that **one difficult question — or even one difficult section — does not define their entire performance**.

## A simple weekly SEAG routine

For many pupils, a manageable week could look like this:

**Monday:** Maths weak topic

**Tuesday:** English comprehension and vocabulary

**Wednesday:** Mixed English and Maths questions

**Thursday:** Maths problem-solving

**Friday:** light review or rest

**Weekend:** timed practice followed by corrections

This does not need to mean hours of revision every day.

Consistent focused practice is usually far more useful than long sessions where concentration disappears.

## Do not forget the official SEAG practice papers

Official SEAG practice materials should form part of preparation because they help children become familiar with the structure of the assessment.

SEAG provides practice papers, answer sheets and guidance for parents through its official resources.

Use them carefully.

Do not burn through every full paper immediately.

A practice paper is most valuable when you **analyse what happened afterwards**.

## Eight weeks can change a lot

A child does not need to become perfect in eight weeks.

They need to become **better than they are today**.

That might mean improving comprehension accuracy.

It might mean becoming faster at Maths.

It might mean learning not to panic when a difficult problem appears.

The pupils who improve most are often not the ones doing the greatest number of questions.

They are the ones who understand their mistakes and stop repeating them.
