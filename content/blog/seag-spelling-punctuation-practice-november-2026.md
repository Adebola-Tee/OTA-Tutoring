SEAG English preparation can become dominated by comprehension passages. Spelling and punctuation deserve attention too, especially when a child repeatedly overlooks a small error despite understanding the sentence. A short, careful practice session can reveal more than another paper completed without reviewing the answers.

The [official SEAG assessment guide](https://seagni.co.uk/guidance/assessment-guide) includes spelling, punctuation, grammar and vocabulary alongside comprehension. The 2026 assessments are on 14 and 21 November, as shown in the [SEAG key dates](https://seagni.co.uk/guidance/key-dates). Use the remaining weeks to strengthen specific habits, rather than attempting to memorise every possible word.

## Find the repeated decision behind the mistake

Look at recent English practice and group errors by their cause. Does your child confuse words that sound alike, put an apostrophe into every word ending in s or stop checking once the sentence sounds reasonable?

These patterns need different teaching. For a homophone, discuss meaning. For an apostrophe, establish whether the word shows possession, a contraction or an ordinary plural. For missed punctuation, ask the child to read the whole sentence and identify its boundaries.

Choose one recurring pattern for the next session. Trying to correct six rules at once makes it harder to tell which one the child can now apply independently.

## Make apostrophes depend on meaning

Compare these original examples: “The girl's coats were wet” and “The girls' coats were wet.” In the first, the coats belong to one girl. In the second, they belong to more than one girl. The position of the apostrophe changes the meaning.

Now try “The girls waited outside.” This is an ordinary plural, so it does not need an apostrophe. Ask your child to explain the difference before choosing punctuation. A useful checking question is “Who owns what?” when possession is involved.

Then compare “it's raining” with “its roof is leaking.” In the first, “it's” means “it is.” In the second, “its” indicates possession without an apostrophe. Replacing “it's” with “it is” can help check whether that form fits.

## Check homophones inside sentences

Give the child this original task: choose the correct word in “They left their bags over there.” Discuss each word's job rather than learning the three spellings as an isolated chant.

“Their” tells us whose bags they are. “There” refers to a place. “They're” would mean “they are,” which does not fit before “bags” in this sentence. Ask the child to make a new sentence for each form.

If they answer correctly with help, revisit the distinction later using different wording. Recognising today's example is not yet evidence that the rule will survive an unfamiliar task.

## Use a short routine with a clear finish

Start with two examples, discuss the reasoning, then ask the child to complete a small set independently. Review why each correction is needed. Finish with one fresh sentence that combines the target rule with ordinary reading.

Keep a simple record: the rule, one corrected example and a later independent check. Avoid filling a notebook with copied spellings that the child cannot use in context. Mix previously taught patterns into future sessions so that practice involves choosing the rule, not merely repeating it.

Use official practice materials to learn the actual assessment format and answer sheet instructions. The examples here teach decisions; they are not copied SEAG questions or predictions of the November papers.

## Keep accuracy in balance with the rest of English

Do not replace reading with punctuation drills. Vocabulary, comprehension and sentence meaning support accurate checking. Our [SEAG comprehension guide](seag-comprehension-tutoring-evidence-inference-2026.html) shows how to link an answer to evidence rather than a guess.

Likewise, avoid promising a particular assessment score from a short list of corrections. Review several attempts and notice whether the child needs fewer prompts, explains choices clearly and applies a rule in a different context.
