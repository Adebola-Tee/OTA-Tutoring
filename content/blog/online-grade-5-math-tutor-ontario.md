An online Grade 5 Math tutor should begin by asking how your child reached an answer. Two pupils can get the same question wrong for completely different reasons. One may misunderstand the quantities. Another may choose the right operation but struggle with a multiplication fact. A useful lesson finds the difference before setting more practice.

Canadian curricula vary by province, so tell the tutor where your child attends school. Ontario publishes its [elementary Mathematics curriculum](https://www.dcp.edu.gov.on.ca/en/curriculum/elementary-mathematics) with grade-specific expectations. Bring the current classroom topic and teacher feedback so lessons support the child's programme rather than assume a single curriculum for all of Canada.

## Check what fractions mean before practising rules

Start with a drawing or number line. If a rectangle is divided into eight equal parts and three are shaded, the shaded amount is 3/8. Ask what the denominator represents and why the parts must be equal. Then compare 3/8 with 1/2 by showing that one half is four eighths.

For a child who already understands that comparison, try 3/8 + 2/8. The result is 5/8 because the size of the parts stays the same while the number of parts increases. If the child says 5/16, return to the model and ask whether the pieces suddenly became smaller.

A tutor should adjust the challenge to the learner's current understanding and school expectations. A quick correct answer is not enough if the child cannot explain what it represents. Equally, a pupil who understands the idea should not spend every lesson repeating an introductory worksheet.

## Connect decimals to place value

Ask which is larger, 0.7 or 0.65. A child may choose 0.65 because 65 is larger than 7. Rewrite 0.7 as 0.70, compare the tenths and hundredths, and show the values on a number line. The explanation should connect the symbols to size.

Then ask the pupil to order 0.6, 0.06 and 0.66. Changing the examples checks whether the reasoning transfers. Money can help introduce hundredths, but use other representations too so the child does not understand decimals only when a dollar sign appears.

## Read the relationships in a word problem

Consider this original task: “A club buys 7 packs of 12 pencils and gives 29 pencils to a class. How many are left?” First find 7 × 12 = 84, then subtract 29 to get 55. Ask what each calculation represents before using it.

If the child adds 7 and 12, a tutor can draw the packs or arrange equal groups. If they form the right calculation but subtract incorrectly, the next task should address subtraction. Naming the first point of difficulty prevents a broad label such as “bad at word problems” from hiding a fixable gap.

## Make online lessons active and visible

Ask how the tutor will see your child's working. A shared whiteboard, paper held to the camera or a clear photo can all help. The important part is that the learner attempts the task and explains decisions while the tutor responds to their thinking.

Discuss the time difference and choose a slot when the child can concentrate. A session immediately after a demanding school day may need a different pace from a weekend lesson. Agree on short practice between sessions and how much parental prompting is appropriate.

## Check improvement without relying on one score

Keep a few early attempts and compare them with fresh questions of similar difficulty later. Look for clearer explanations, more reliable methods and less need for reminders. Repeating an identical worksheet may show memory rather than a stronger understanding.

Share classroom feedback with the tutor as it changes. If fractions improve but a new unit introduces measurement difficulties, the plan should respond. Progress needs a direction, but it should not become a rigid schedule that ignores school work.
