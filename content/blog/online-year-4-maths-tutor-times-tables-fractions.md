An **online Year 4 Maths tutor** can help a child who is finding number work difficult. Tutoring can also challenge a child who enjoys Maths but wants to understand ideas more deeply. The important question is what the child can *explain*, not only how quickly they answer.

## Start with a short picture of what your child knows

Ask the child to work through three kinds of question: a times-table fact, a simple fraction of a quantity, and a word problem using the same numbers. For example, they might know that 6 × 4 = 24 but struggle with “six packets contain four pencils each.” That tells you the facts are there, while choosing an operation needs work.

England's [Year 4 mathematics curriculum](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/national-curriculum-in-england-mathematics-programmes-of-study/national-curriculum-in-england-mathematics-programmes-of-study) covers multiplication and division, fractions, measurement and reasoning. If your child follows a different curriculum, show the tutor the school scheme of work.

## Times tables should support thinking

Quick recall matters, but a child should also see *why* 7 × 6 equals 42. Arrange seven groups of six counters, draw an array, or use 5 × 6 plus 2 × 6. Those approaches give the learner a route back when a fact slips their mind.

Try asking “How could you work out 8 × 7 without counting every object?” A tutor who listens to the answer can decide whether to practise recall, model an array or revisit doubling.

## Fractions need a visible whole

A child may confidently say “a quarter is one of four” while being unable to find a quarter of 20. Show 20 objects divided into four equal groups. Ask what the whole is, how many groups it has and how many objects each group contains. Then remove the picture and try a new amount.

This is a good model for an online lesson: explain with a shared drawing, let the learner move or mark the groups, and ask them to solve the next question alone. A screen should make the thinking visible, not turn the child into an audience.

## Questions worth asking a tutor

- How will you distinguish a memory lapse from a missing concept?
- Will you use the child's current school work alongside your own questions?
- What short practice should happen between lessons?
- How will you show progress in reasoning as well as speed?

## A five-minute activity to try today

Ask your child to find two ways to make 24 using multiplication. Then ask them to show one quarter of 24 with objects or a drawing. If they say six, ask them to explain why four equal groups of six make the whole 24. If they cannot, show the groups together and ask them to try again with 12.

This little activity shows more than a worksheet score: whether the child connects facts, quantities and visual representations. Tell a prospective tutor what you noticed, including any part your child found enjoyable. A good lesson can build on a strength while addressing a gap.
