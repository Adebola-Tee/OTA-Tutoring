Three practice tests, almost the same score. It is tempting to buy another question bank and hope the next one changes things. Before spending more time or money, look at what your child is doing between tests. If the same misunderstanding survives each review, a new paper merely measures it again.

Improving an ACT Math score starts with a more precise question. Which decisions are costing points, and what would help the student make those decisions differently? ## Sort missed questions by the first wrong step

Keep the original working. A topic label such as algebra is useful, but it does not explain the mistake. Did the student build the wrong equation, distribute a negative incorrectly, misunderstand a graph or answer a different question from the one asked?

For example, consider 4(x − 2) = 20. A learner who writes 4x − 2 = 20 needs help with multiplication across brackets. Someone who reaches x = 7 but selects 20 because that number appears in the question has a different problem. The right follow-up lesson depends on the distinction.

Ask your child to explain the point where their solution stopped making sense. If they cannot locate it, work backwards through the answer rather than immediately copying the correct method.

## Include correct answers that were guesses

A correct response may hide an uncertain method. Mark guessed answers and questions where the student needed far too long, even when the final selection was right. These belong in the review too.

Ask for a fresh example after an explanation. If the learner can solve only the original question, the improvement may be recognition of a remembered answer. Change a coefficient, switch from a graph to a table, or ask what a parameter means. That small change can expose what still needs teaching.

## Compare timed and untimed performance

Use a short set of unfamiliar questions without a clock, followed on another occasion by a comparable timed set. Do not treat two different sets as a perfectly controlled experiment; use the contrast as a clue.

If the same errors occur in both, time pressure is probably not the only issue. If the untimed work is accurate but timed work becomes disorganised, inspect method choice, reading habits and time spent on difficult questions. Our guide to [running out of time in ACT Math](act-math-running-out-of-time.html) explores those decisions in more detail.

## Practise one repair before another full test

Choose a repeated difficulty and give it a clear short-term goal. A student struggling with function notation might practise finding inputs and outputs, then interpreting the same relationship in a table. A student mishandling percentages needs different examples.

Finish with mixed questions that do not announce the method. Knowing what to do on a worksheet labelled linear equations is easier than recognising a linear relationship among several topics. That recognition matters in the test.

Keep a brief record of what changed. Fewer repeated setup errors is useful evidence even before the overall score moves. Another full test makes more sense once there has been teaching, independent practice and a check of the repaired skill.

## Use practice that matches the current test

The enhanced ACT Math section has 45 questions in 50 minutes under standard timing. Older resources may still contain useful mathematics, but their pacing and scoring should not automatically be applied to the current format. Use [official ACT practice materials](https://www.act.org/content/act/en/products-and-services/the-act/test-preparation/free-act-test-prep.html) and the scoring guide supplied with the particular test.

Bring that evidence into your tutoring decision. You do not need a perfect explanation of every weakness before seeking help. A recent paper, the student's working and a realistic test date are enough to begin a useful conversation about the next steps.
