The homework used to take twenty minutes. Now your child spends an hour staring at it, asks you to explain every question and says the teacher is moving too quickly. You do not need to wait for a failed exam before looking more closely.

Falling behind in maths usually needs a specific response, not a judgement about ability. The useful question is whether a difficulty is temporary or whether a missing skill is making each new lesson harder. A tutor can help with the second problem, but the first conversation should establish what is actually happening.

## Look for a pattern across several pieces of work

One poor result after illness or a difficult new topic does not prove a lasting gap. Compare classwork, homework and the teacher's observations. Does the same problem recur when the numbers or context change?

A child might manage addition but struggle whenever regrouping is needed. An older learner might follow algebra until fractions appear. These are more useful findings than saying the child is weak at maths.

Ask the school which prerequisite matters most for the current topic. You can also ask whether the student begins independently or waits for help. Those observations help distinguish a knowledge gap from unfamiliar instructions or an unsuitable starting task.

## Check the foundation beneath the current lesson

Suppose a student struggles with 3/4 of 20. Teaching a longer fraction method may be premature if they cannot divide 20 into four equal groups. The starting point could be equal sharing and multiplication, not a worksheet of increasingly difficult fractions.

For a secondary student unable to solve 5x + 7 = 22, inspect inverse operations and the meaning of equality before moving to equations with brackets. A lesson that meets the learner at the right point can prevent unnecessary frustration.

The [Education Endowment Foundation's maths guidance](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/guidance-reports/maths-ks-2-3) emphasises understanding existing knowledge when planning teaching. The practical lesson for parents is to seek an explanation of the gap, rather than simply buying more revision material.

## Notice when support has become dependence

Helping with every first step can make completed homework look reassuring while concealing what the child can do alone. Try one suitable question without prompts, then ask for an explanation. Keep the atmosphere calm; this is information gathering, not another test to pass.

If a learner understands after an explanation but cannot begin a fresh example later, regular teaching may be needed. Our article on [maths at home versus school tests](child-understands-maths-at-home-but-fails-tests.html) explores why prompted practice can feel easier than independent work.

Avoid making the task deliberately difficult to prove a point. Start with something connected to the current school topic and watch where the reasoning breaks down.

## Get help before the workload becomes the problem

It may be sensible to arrange tutoring when gaps persist despite appropriate school support, homework repeatedly causes distress, or new topics depend on knowledge the child has not secured. Discuss the pattern with the teacher as well as the tutor.

Online lessons should connect with the student's curriculum and allow the teacher to see their working. The plan should include independent practice and a way to review whether help is gradually becoming less necessary.
