Last week your child solved every question after the explanation. Today they look at the same kind of work and say they have never learned it. It can feel as though the lesson disappeared overnight.

There are several possible reasons. The child may have copied a sequence without understanding it, relied on prompts, or had too few chances to retrieve the idea later. A useful response is to check what was learned and how it was practised, rather than accuse the learner of not paying attention.

## Test understanding beyond the lesson example

Suppose a child learns to subtract 38 from 62. They complete similar questions with help, but cannot explain why regrouping changes the written digits without changing the value of 62.

Use tens and ones or a suitable drawing to make that visible. Then ask for a different example. If the child can carry out a memorised procedure only when the layout is identical, it may not yet be securely connected to place value.

For an older student, ask why the same operation is applied to both sides of an equation. Repeating the phrase move it over does not show whether equality is understood. A brief explanation can reveal where reteaching is needed.

## Reduce prompts gradually

Help during a lesson should make later independence possible. First, the teacher may demonstrate a method. Next, the child completes an example with questions rather than instructions. Finally, they attempt a fresh problem without either.

If an adult always announces the first step, the child never has to retrieve it. Completed homework can then exaggerate what they know alone. Try asking what they notice before telling them which operation to use.

Allow a little thinking time. Immediately rescuing the learner from every pause can make ordinary retrieval feel like failure. If there is genuine confusion, offer a small cue and note what it was; that information helps plan the next lesson.

## Revisit the idea after a gap

Instead of completing twenty nearly identical questions in one sitting and abandoning the topic, use a smaller amount of practice and return to it later. Include a question from an earlier lesson alongside the current work.

A simple routine might be two new questions, one recent question and one older question. Adjust the difficulty so this is achievable. The purpose is to practise retrieving and selecting methods, not to turn every evening into a test.

The [EEF's maths teaching guidance](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/guidance-reports/maths-ks-2-3) is a useful source on developing mathematical knowledge and monitoring what pupils understand. A teacher or tutor should use that information to decide when a skill needs revisiting.

## Mix examples once the method is understood

A page labelled multiplication announces what to do. A mixed set makes the learner identify whether the situation calls for multiplication, division or another relationship. That choice is part of learning.

For example, compare six bags of four apples with six apples followed by four more. The numbers are the same; the structures are different. Ask the child to explain the distinction before calculating.
