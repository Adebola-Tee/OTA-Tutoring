A child says one eighth is bigger than one quarter because eight is bigger than four. The answer reveals a useful misunderstanding. They are comparing the numbers as whole numbers without considering what the denominator describes.

Before adding more fraction exercises, return to the idea of a whole divided into equal parts. Fraction teaching works better when the child can explain those parts, compare them and locate the amount. Rules for calculation should grow from that understanding.

## Establish what counts as one whole

Draw two identical rectangles. Divide one into four equal pieces and the other into eight equal pieces. Shade one piece in each. A quarter of the same-sized whole is larger than an eighth because fewer equal parts make each part larger.

Then change the size of one rectangle. Half of a large cake can be bigger than a whole small cake. That does not mean one half is a number larger than one; the real objects have different whole sizes.

Keep the whole clear when comparing quantities. Ask your child what the fraction is a fraction of before discussing which shaded region looks bigger.

## Check that the parts are equal

A drawing with three pieces is not necessarily divided into thirds. The pieces must be equal in size. Let the child fold paper, compare strips or share objects to test that condition.

For fractions of a collection, separate twelve counters into four equal groups. Each group contains three, so one quarter of twelve is three. Two quarters contain six. The denominator tells how many equal groups to make, while the numerator tells how many of those groups are being considered.

Do not insist on symbolic calculation before the child can describe this process. A concrete model can make the notation meaningful rather than merely memorable.

## Show fractions as numbers too

A child who recognises a shaded pizza may still struggle to place three quarters on a number line. Draw a line from zero to one, split the interval into four equal lengths and count three of them from zero.

Now extend the line to two. Five quarters lies beyond one, at one and a quarter. This helps prevent the assumption that every fraction must represent less than a whole.

Use the same fraction in a strip, a collection and on a number line. Ask what is shared across the representations. The amount and equal-part relationship matter more than the picture's decoration.

## Explain equivalence before shortcuts

Two quarters and one half cover the same length on equal-sized strips. Split every quarter into two equal pieces and two quarters becomes four eighths. The pieces changed, but the amount did not.

That is why multiplying numerator and denominator by the same positive whole number can produce an equivalent fraction. Adding the same number to both does not generally preserve the value.

The [national curriculum mathematics programme](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/national-curriculum-in-england-mathematics-programmes-of-study/national-curriculum-in-england-mathematics-programmes-of-study) provides a reference for UK school expectations. Follow the child's own curriculum when deciding the next calculation skill; a year label alone does not establish readiness.

## Choose a small next step

If equal sharing is uncertain, work there first. If sharing is secure but comparison is weak, use strips and number lines. If representations are understood but arithmetic breaks down, inspect multiplication and division facts.
