A disappointing Physics result can make two lessons a week sound immediately better than one. Before choosing the schedule, find out what those additional sessions would teach. More time can help, but it cannot compensate for a plan aimed at the wrong difficulty.

## Find the repeated obstacle

Look at several missed questions and identify where the solution first went wrong. A student might confuse mass and weight, choose the wrong equation or give a written answer that omits a causal explanation.

For instance, weight is the gravitational force on an object, while mass describes a different physical quantity. A learner who substitutes a mass directly as a force may need the relationship explained before attempting more mechanics calculations.

That is a different priority from understanding the model but struggling to rearrange an equation. The first lessons should follow the evidence rather than a general label that the learner is weak in Science.

## Keep the qualification and tier in view

Confirm whether the student takes separate Physics or Combined Science, the board and the tier. Ask which topics school has covered and what the next assessment includes.

The [AQA Physics course overview](https://www.aqa.org.uk/subjects/physics/gcse/physics-8463/specification/specification-at-a-glance) is a reference for its separate GCSE. A learner on another course needs appropriately matched resources. Topic overlap does not justify assuming identical content or assessment demands.

Our guide to [Combined Science and separate Physics support](combined-science-or-separate-physics-tutoring.html) explains why that detail affects the plan. The same nominal number of lessons may serve different priorities for different entries.

## Give sessions distinct purposes

One regular appointment may suit a focused difficulty when the student has time for short practice between lessons. More frequent teaching may be worth discussing when several prerequisites are blocking current work or a deadline creates a tightly limited preparation period.

If an extra session is suggested, ask what it adds. It might separate conceptual teaching from reviewing independent responses, but it should not simply repeat the same explanations more often.

A student who has no time to attempt anything alone gives the tutor limited evidence about retention. The schedule needs space for the learner to select an equation, interpret data or write an explanation without a teacher's prompts.

## Balance Physics with the rest of the week

GCSE students are preparing several subjects. List school homework, other lessons and realistic practice periods before committing to an intensive plan. A sustainable schedule makes it easier to use feedback thoughtfully.

For a learner anxious about mocks, ask the school about dates and coverage. Schools arrange mocks differently. A tutor should not claim to predict the questions or guarantee a particular result by adding a certain number of hours.

As the assessment approaches, choose priorities that the student's work supports. Maintaining secure skills matters too; the plan should not become a constant search for increasingly difficult questions.

## Review frequency as the learning changes

Agree an initial goal and a review point. Useful evidence includes clearer force diagrams, fewer repeated unit errors or an explanation that connects the correct scientific ideas without prompting.

If the same uncertainty remains, ask whether the explanation, practice or starting priority needs changing before buying more sessions. More of an ineffective routine is not a complete response to limited progress.
