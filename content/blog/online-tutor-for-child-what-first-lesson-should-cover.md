“I need an **online tutor for my child**” is a perfectly good starting point. You do not need a complete diagnosis before asking for help. You *do* need a clear conversation about the child's age, curriculum, subject and goal, whether that is keeping up in Year 3 or preparing for an international exam.

## Before the call: collect three things

First, note what the learner is studying now. Second, find one recent piece of work or test that shows what happens when they try independently. Third, ask your child what feels easy and what feels difficult. A student may want a challenge rather than help recovering a weak grade; that goal matters too.

## What should happen in the first lesson?

The tutor should put the student at ease, check one or two relevant skills and teach something useful. They should watch *how* the child thinks: whether the problem is understanding a concept, reading a question, expressing an answer or working under time pressure.

The [Education Endowment Foundation's one-to-one tuition overview](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/teaching-learning-toolkit/one-to-one-tuition) describes the value of matching teaching to a pupil's understanding and providing interaction and feedback. A child should not spend the whole session passively watching a slideshow.

## How do you judge the fit?

Ask the child to explain one idea from the lesson afterwards. Ask the tutor what they noticed, what the next priority is and how progress will be checked. A good answer describes the learner's work, rather than guaranteeing a grade or selling a fixed package before seeing the child.

For younger learners, check that they can stay engaged and use the online tools. For older students, confirm the exact course, level or exam board. If Maths is your priority, these [questions to ask an online Maths tutor](https://otalearningstudio.com/blog/how-to-choose-online-maths-tutor-questions-parents-should-ask.html) are a useful companion.
