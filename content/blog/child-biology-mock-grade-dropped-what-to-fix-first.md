A lower **GCSE Biology mock grade** can feel alarming, especially when your child seemed to be making progress.

But one disappointing result does not automatically mean they have forgotten everything or are suddenly “bad at Biology”.

Mocks expose different problems. The important question is not simply:

**“Why did the grade drop?”**

It is:

**“Where exactly did the marks disappear?”**

That answer should determine what your child fixes first.

## First, compare the marks — not only the grades

Start by comparing the latest mock with your child’s previous Biology test.

A grade can fall because the paper was harder or covered weaker topics. Look at the actual marks and question types.

Ask:

- Which topics appeared most frequently?
- Were several questions left unfinished?
- Did your child lose marks on calculations, practicals or extended answers?
- Were the mistakes concentrated in one topic or spread across the paper?
- Did time pressure affect the final section?

This prevents a broad response such as, “Revise all of Biology again.”

Most students do not need to restart the entire course. They need to repair the weaknesses revealed by the mock.

## Separate content gaps from exam-technique problems

Take the marked paper and place every lost mark into one of these categories:

**The content was not known:** your child could not recall or explain the relevant Biology.

**The content was known but not applied:** they understood the topic at home but could not connect it to an unfamiliar question.

**The answer was too vague:** they had the correct idea but did not use enough precise scientific language.

**The question was misread:** they missed the command word, ignored data or answered something slightly different.

**There was a practical, mathematical or graph error:** the Biology knowledge may have been secure, but another skill caused the lost marks.

Whichever category contains the most marks should become the first priority.

## Fix repeated topic gaps first

One incorrect answer does not always prove that an entire topic is weak. Repeated mistakes do.

Your child may be losing marks across several questions involving cell transport, enzymes, infection and response, bioenergetics, homeostasis, inheritance or ecology.

Choose the two or three areas responsible for the greatest loss — not ten topics at once.

For each weak area, your child should:

1. Review the key concept.
2. Explain it without copying from notes.
3. Answer focused exam questions.
4. Check the mark scheme.
5. Rewrite vague or incomplete answers.

This is more effective than repeatedly reading a revision guide and assuming the topic is secure.

## Check the command words

A student can know the Biology and still answer the wrong question.

Words such as **describe, explain, compare, calculate, suggest** and **evaluate** require different responses.

For example, a “describe” question may ask your child to state what the data shows. An “explain” question requires a reason using scientific knowledge.

Before answering, they should underline the command word and identify exactly what the examiner wants.

## Do not ignore practical and data questions

Required practicals, graphs, tables and calculations can quietly pull down an otherwise strong Biology grade.

Check whether your child struggled with:

- identifying variables
- describing methods
- suggesting improvements
- calculating magnification or percentage change
- interpreting graphs
- drawing conclusions from results

These questions require repeated practice with exam-style data and practical scenarios.

## Was timing part of the problem?

Look at the final pages.

If the early answers are detailed but later responses are rushed, incomplete or blank, the issue may be exam timing rather than Biology knowledge.

Your child should practise completing short sections under timed conditions. They do not need to sit a full paper every day to improve their speed.

## What should your child fix first?

Use this order:

1. **Repeated content gaps** affecting several questions
2. **Application and command-word mistakes**
3. **Required practicals, data and calculations**
4. **Timing and careless errors**

After one or two weeks, give your child fresh questions on the same weak areas. Improvement should be measured through new answers, not by how many hours they spent revising.
