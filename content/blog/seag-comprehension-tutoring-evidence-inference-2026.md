A child can read a passage smoothly and still find SEAG comprehension difficult. Reading the words, locating a detail and deciding what a character's actions suggest are different skills. If your P7 child keeps choosing an answer that sounds plausible, another whole practice paper may not show them how to choose more carefully.

The [official SEAG key dates](https://seagni.co.uk/guidance/key-dates) place the 2026 assessments on 14 and 21 November. There is still time to practise a clearer reading routine. Comprehension tutoring should show the pupil how to connect an answer to the passage, then check whether they can do that without prompts.

## Start with what the question actually asks

Before looking at the options, ask the child to put the question into their own words. Does it ask for a stated detail, the meaning of a word in context or an inference about someone's feelings? These tasks need different kinds of evidence.

For a stated detail, return to the relevant line and check the exact information. For an inference, consider what the action or description suggests. Avoid turning every question into a search for a matching word: an option can repeat a word from the passage and still answer the wrong question.

## Use a small passage to make inference visible

Try this original example: “Leah reached the classroom door, checked the folded note in her pocket and waited until the corridor was empty before knocking.” Ask why Leah might be hesitant. The checking, waiting and delayed knock support uncertainty or nervousness. They do not prove that she has stolen something or dislikes her teacher.

Have the child name the clue and explain its connection to their answer. “She is nervous because she waits and checks the note” is more useful than “I just think she is.” Also discuss what remains unknown. Reading carefully includes knowing when the passage does not give enough evidence for a strong claim.

Now alter the passage so Leah strides in and hands over the note immediately. Ask how the interpretation changes. Comparing two versions helps a pupil see that inference comes from details rather than from a story they invent around the character.

## Teach vocabulary inside the sentence

When a word is unfamiliar, read the surrounding sentence and consider the situation. In “After the long climb, Amir was reluctant to leave the shady bench,” the setting suggests that he does not want to leave. It does not mean that he cannot leave.

Ask the child to replace “reluctant” with a simpler phrase and reread the sentence. Does the meaning still fit? Then test the word in a new context, such as being reluctant to apologise. This is a better check than recognising a definition once on a vocabulary list.

## Review wrong answers without doing the thinking for them

When marking a question, ask which clue made an option attractive and which detail rules it out. An answer may be too extreme, contradict the passage or describe something possible but unsupported. Give the pupil time to explain before showing the correct choice.

Keep a short record of the reason for each mistake. “Misread who spoke” and “guessed an unfamiliar word” need different practice. Our guide to [choosing an online SEAG English tutor](online-seag-english-tutor-northern-ireland-2026.html) explains how lessons can respond to those differences.

## Move from careful reading to sensible pacing

Begin with short passages and accurate reasoning. Once the child can explain choices, introduce a small timed set. Review whether the difficulty was finding evidence, understanding vocabulary or spending too long between two options.

Practise moving on from a difficult item and returning when time allows. Keep the practice calm and specific. A child who feels every mistake is a prediction of their final result may become more hesitant, even when their reading is improving.
