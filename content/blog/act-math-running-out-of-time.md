The last questions are blank, but your child can solve several of them afterwards. That is frustrating evidence of lost opportunity. It does not necessarily mean the answer is to work faster at everything. Often, a handful of expensive decisions earlier in the section has used the time needed elsewhere.

## Find the questions that consumed the clock

During a practice section, record a few checkpoints rather than interrupting every question to time it. Afterwards, identify where progress slowed. Was the student stuck reading a long problem, repeatedly recalculating, or continuing with an algebraic method that had become unwieldy?

The enhanced ACT Math section allows 50 minutes for 45 questions under standard timing. That average is a planning reference, not an instruction to spend exactly the same time on every item. Some questions take seconds; others reasonably need longer. Check the [current ACT test structure](https://www.act.org/content/act/en/products-and-services/the-act/test-preparation/act-exam-sections-and-structure.html) rather than using an old 60-question pacing plan.

## Separate slow recall from slow decisions

A learner who takes a long time to calculate 15% of 80 may need stronger percentage fluency. Someone who calculates quickly but rereads the problem five times may need practice translating the situation into mathematics.

Try the question again without a clock and ask for an explanation of the first step. If the method is still unclear, timed drills alone are unlikely to repair it. Teach the missing connection first, then gradually introduce a realistic time limit.

If familiar questions are quick but unfamiliar wording causes a pause, practise spotting the requested quantity. Underline what must be found and write a short expression before carrying out the calculation.

## Choose a workable route before calculating

For a question asking which number satisfies an equation, testing answer choices may sometimes be efficient. For a simple equation, ordinary algebra may be quicker. The student needs to recognise when each route is sensible.

Suppose 3x + 4 = 19. Solving directly gives x = 5 in two steps; lengthy calculator work adds little. A more complicated numerical comparison may benefit from a permitted calculator. Teach method choice using the actual question, not a rule that every item should be solved in one particular way.

Show written working clearly enough to avoid restarting. Repeatedly reconstructing a half-finished calculation is an unnecessary time cost.

## Practise moving on deliberately

A student can feel that leaving a question is admitting defeat. Reframe it as protecting time for questions they can answer. If an approach has stalled and no useful next step is appearing, make a sensible selection where possible, mark the item and move on.

Return if time remains. Practise that behaviour before test day so it becomes a decision rather than a panic response. Avoid an inflexible rule based only on the question number; use the learner's actual performance to set checkpoints.

## Check progress on unfamiliar work

A faster second attempt at the same paper is not strong evidence that pacing has improved. The student already knows the questions. Use a fresh comparable set after practising a specific change, such as recording the target quantity or leaving stalled items sooner.

Review accuracy alongside speed. Finishing everything with rushed guesses is not the goal. Look for more questions attempted thoughtfully, fewer unnecessary restarts and a calmer plan for difficult items.
