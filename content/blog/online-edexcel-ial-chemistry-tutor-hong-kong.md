If you are looking for an online Edexcel IAL Chemistry tutor in Hong Kong, begin with the unit your child is taking and a question they cannot yet explain. “Chemistry is difficult” might mean confusing organic reactions, losing marks in calculations or giving answers that are correct in general but too vague for the question.

These difficulties need different teaching. A useful first conversation should identify which one is limiting progress, then connect lessons to the learner's school course and examination plans.

## Check the specification before choosing lessons

For students taking the current course, Pearson's [2018 International A Level Chemistry specification](https://qualifications.pearson.com/en/qualifications/edexcel-international-advanced-levels/chemistry-2018.html) is the starting point. The newer Chemistry qualification is for first teaching from September 2027; a January 2027 candidate should not switch resources merely because the newer page has “2027” in its title.

Send the tutor the unit codes shown on the entry confirmation or school course information. WCH11 and WCH14, for example, cover different stages of the course. Someone retaking one unit needs a different plan from a student preparing several units for the first time.

## Make organic reactions more than a memory list

Consider an original lesson example comparing two reactions of bromoethane. With aqueous hydroxide, substitution can form ethanol. With ethanolic hydroxide and heating, elimination can form ethene. Remembering the product names is only the beginning.

Ask the student to explain what happens to the carbon skeleton and why the conditions matter. Then give a different halogenoalkane and ask them to predict a product. Where their entered unit requires mechanisms, check charges, electron movement and the direction of curly arrows as well.

This reveals whether they understand the reaction or recognise a familiar diagram. A useful tutor moves between names, structures, conditions and explanations instead of asking the learner to copy another reaction map.

## Ask what every calculation represents

Take this simple starting task. A solution contains 0.0250 mol of solute in 100 cm³. Convert the volume to 0.100 dm³, then divide amount by volume to obtain 0.250 mol per dm³. Ask the student what those numbers represent before making the problem harder.

In a reacting mixture, the balanced equation adds another decision: which mole ratio connects the known substance to the unknown one? A tutor should check that decision explicitly. Correct calculator use cannot repair an incorrect ratio.

Keep units attached to the working and estimate whether the final answer is sensible. If the result changes dramatically when the student converts a volume, ask them to explain why. ## Turn vague explanations into chemical reasoning

Suppose a learner explains a boiling point difference by writing “the forces are stronger.” Ask which particles and which forces they mean. Are they discussing intermolecular attractions or bonds within a molecule? What energy change explains the observation?

That discussion matters because familiar phrases can hide an incorrect model. The student should compare the relevant structures, name the interaction and connect it to the property. Practise on an unfamiliar pair rather than rewarding a memorised sentence that happens to fit one example.

## Make online lessons active and practical

A learner should spend part of the session writing, drawing or solving independently while the tutor observes their reasoning. Shared notes are useful, but watching someone produce a complete answer is not the same as producing one yourself.

Agree lesson times in Hong Kong time and confirm how homework and feedback will work. Fit the routine around school, other subjects and the units actually entered. Ask what evidence of progress will be reviewed after the first few sessions, such as fewer prompts or clearer explanations on fresh questions.
