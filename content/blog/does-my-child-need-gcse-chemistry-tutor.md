Every GCSE student finds some Chemistry topics difficult.

That alone does not mean your child needs a tutor.

But there is a difference between:

**“I don’t understand electrolysis yet.”**

and:

**“I don’t understand Chemistry anymore.”**

If the problem has started spreading across topics, tests and confidence, it may be time to look more closely.

## 1. One difficult topic has turned into several

Perhaps the problem started with bonding.

Then quantitative Chemistry became difficult.

Then moles made no sense.

Then electrolysis arrived and everything felt worse.

Chemistry builds on earlier ideas, so gaps can travel.

If your child never became secure with **atomic structure, bonding or chemical equations**, later topics can feel much harder than they should.

Our guide on [where to start when your child is struggling with GCSE Chemistry](https://otalearningstudio.com/blog/child-struggling-gcse-chemistry-where-to-start.html) explains why fixing the foundations first can make such a difference.

## 2. They understand the lesson but cannot answer exam questions

This is one of the clearest signs that extra support may help.

Your child says:

**“I understood it when the teacher explained it.”**

But then the test question looks different and they do not know how to start.

That usually means the knowledge is not yet secure enough to apply independently.

A good tutor should help bridge that gap between:

**recognising an explanation**

and

**solving an unfamiliar GCSE question alone.**

## 3. Chemistry calculations are becoming a major weakness

Some students are comfortable with the science but lose large numbers of marks whenever calculations appear.

Watch for problems with:

* relative formula mass
* moles
* concentration
* percentages
* balanced equations
* unit conversions
* standard form

If calculation mistakes keep appearing across several topics, the issue may be bigger than one Chemistry chapter.

Our guide on [why GCSE Chemistry calculations can pull a student’s grade down](https://otalearningstudio.com/blog/gcse-chemistry-calculations-pulling-grade-down.html) explains how to identify exactly where those marks are being lost.

## 4. Their grades have stopped moving

A student may be revising regularly but still receive almost the same result every time.

That is worth paying attention to.

It can mean they are:

* practising the wrong topics
* rereading instead of applying
* avoiding difficult questions
* repeating the same misconceptions
* failing to review mistakes properly

More revision hours will not automatically solve that.

Sometimes the student needs someone to look at their work and identify the pattern they cannot see themselves.

## 5. They cannot explain *why* something happens

Chemistry becomes much harder when students rely entirely on memorised sentences.

For example, they may remember:

**“Ionic bonding involves electron transfer.”**

But can they explain:

* which particles are involved?
* why ions form?
* why opposite charges attract?
* how this affects the properties of the substance?

If your child can repeat definitions but struggles when the question changes, they may not understand the concept deeply enough yet.

That will keep showing up in exam questions.

## 6. They avoid practical and longer-answer questions

Some students are reasonably comfortable with short recall questions but struggle badly with:

**Explain**

**Evaluate**

**Describe a method**

**Suggest an improvement**

Required practicals and scientific reasoning are an important part of GCSE Chemistry.

If these questions are consistently left blank or answered vaguely, the student may need help learning how to turn what they know into clear, mark-worthy answers.

## 7. Confidence has started to disappear

Listen to the language your child uses.

**“Chemistry is impossible.”**

**“I’ll never understand moles.”**

**“I’m just bad at Science.”**

Once that mindset settles in, students often stop attempting questions properly.

They guess.

They leave blanks.

They avoid the subject.

At that point, support is not only about content.

It is about helping the student start experiencing small, genuine successes again.

## 8. They are working hard — but the mistakes never change

This is probably the biggest sign.

Your child studies.

They watch videos.

They complete worksheets.

But the same errors keep returning.

That means the revision is not solving the real problem.

Instead of asking:

**“How many hours did you revise?”**

ask:

**“What mistake did you stop making this week?”**

That tells you much more.

## What should a good Chemistry tutor actually do?

A tutor should not simply open the textbook at Chapter 1.

They should first find out:

* which exam board your child takes
* whether they do Combined or Separate Science
* current and target grades
* weakest topics
* whether calculations are a problem
* whether the issue is knowledge, application or exam technique

Then lessons should be built around those findings.

That is what makes tutoring useful.

Not simply adding another Chemistry lesson to the week.

## So, does your child actually need a Chemistry tutor?

Possibly — when the problem is no longer one difficult topic and your child is **not closing the gap independently**.

You do not need to wait until mocks go badly.

And you do not need to panic after one poor test either.

Look for patterns.

Repeated confusion.

Repeated low scores.

Repeated calculation errors.

Repeated loss of confidence.

Those patterns tell you far more than one grade.
