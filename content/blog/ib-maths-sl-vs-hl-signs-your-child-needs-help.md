“Everyone finds IB Maths hard at first.” That can be reassuring advice, but it can also hide a problem. A student who needs extra time to learn a new idea is adjusting. A student who cannot follow the next lesson because the previous three never made sense is falling behind.

The difference is not whether homework feels difficult. It is whether understanding is developing. Parents can spot that difference without being able to teach the mathematics themselves.

## Watch the direction of progress

Over two or three weeks, notice what happens to the same kind of task. Does your child need fewer hints? Can they explain a method they could not explain last week? Are corrections helping with new examples? Improvement may be gradual, but it should become visible.

Warning signs look different. The learner copies worked solutions, avoids starting questions, leaves increasing amounts unfinished or studies for longer while understanding less. A single tough assignment is not proof of a widening gap. A repeated pattern across homework, lessons and assessments deserves attention.

## SL and HL demand different study plans

Standard Level and Higher Level are not merely two labels on the same workload. HL requires additional mathematical depth and content. The [official IB mathematics information](https://ibo.org/programmes/diploma-programme/curriculum/mathematics/) sets out the four AA and AI course options. The exact course matters when assessing whether a student’s preparation is adequate.

For example, a learner may handle a direct differentiation question but struggle when the same technique appears inside a longer argument. Extra hours doing the direct questions will not necessarily prepare them for that demand. They need practice linking ideas and choosing a route through an unfamiliar problem.

A learner in SL may instead be losing marks because earlier fraction, algebra or function skills are unreliable. That needs attention too. Difficulty at SL does not mean the student is incapable; it tells us where the teaching should begin.

## Ask questions that reveal independence

Choose a recently corrected question and ask your child to explain the first decision. Why did they use that method? What did the symbols mean? How would the approach change if the numbers or context changed?

An explanation does not need to be polished. You are listening for ownership of the thinking. If the answer is only “that is what the solution did”, the student may have followed the example without learning how to begin independently.

Also ask how they study. Reading notes until they seem familiar is different from attempting a question without help. A manageable routine includes both learning the idea and retrieving it later.

## Bring the school into the conversation early

Ask the teacher which prerequisite skills are weakest, whether the difficulty is typical for this stage and what improvement would look like over the next month. Include the learner in that discussion. Quietly arranging more work without explaining its purpose can feel like a punishment.

If an HL decision needs reviewing, discuss timing, university plans and catch-up requirements with the school. ## Review support using fresh work

After a few weeks, look for less prompting, clearer explanations and better completion on questions the student has not seen before. Those are useful signs that the extra lessons are working.

The aim is not to make every IB task easy. It is to help your child experience difficulty they can work through, instead of a growing backlog they cannot understand. That distinction makes early support worthwhile.
