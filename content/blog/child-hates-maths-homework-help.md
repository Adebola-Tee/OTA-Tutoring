The Maths book comes out, and the argument begins before the first question. It is easy to assume your child dislikes effort or simply needs firmer rules. Yet resistance can also be a sign that the task feels impossible, unclear or too demanding after a long school day.

Start by finding out what happens at the point of refusal. That will tell you more than another reminder to finish the page. The aim is to make learning possible, not to win an evening-long battle over completed boxes.

## Ask about the first question rather than the whole page

Choose one suitable item and ask what your child thinks it means. Can they read the instruction, identify the target and suggest a first step? Avoid asking all three at once if that adds pressure.

If they can explain the task but cannot calculate, inspect the mathematical skill. If the calculation is manageable but the wording is confusing, clarify the instruction and ask for a similar example. These are different kinds of help.

A long worksheet can feel overwhelming even when individual questions are accessible. Agree a small starting section and a sensible pause, while keeping the teacher's expectations in mind. Splitting the work does not require pretending it has been completed.

## Check whether the difficulty begins earlier

A child asked to add fractions may not yet understand equivalent fractions. Someone solving equations might still be uncertain about negative numbers. Repeating the current task without repairing that foundation can make resistance more understandable.

Our article on [fractions foundations](child-confused-by-fractions-foundations.html) shows how to investigate one common gap. Use a model or a simpler related question to identify the missing connection, rather than replacing the whole assignment with unrelated easy work.

If homework regularly depends on ideas the child has not secured, tell the teacher what you observed. A specific example is more useful than reporting that the child hates all Maths.

## Keep help honest and limited

An adult supplying each answer may get the page finished but hide what the student needs taught. Demonstrate one related example if necessary, then let the child attempt the actual question. Note how much support was needed.

Avoid silently changing the school's method when that creates further confusion. Ask the teacher how the calculation is being represented, particularly for a younger learner. Understanding a method is more valuable than copying a different shortcut that the child cannot explain.

The [primary Maths teaching guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/teaching-mathematics-in-primary-schools) provides useful context for UK number work. Match support to the child's curriculum rather than assuming every school teaches the same sequence in the same way.

## Make the routine easier to begin

Choose a realistic time and have the basic materials ready. Ask whether the child needs a short break after school before concentrating. Reducing practical friction can help you see more clearly whether the remaining difficulty is mathematical.

Use feedback that notices a decision. You drew equal groups before multiplying is more informative than you are a genius. It gives the learner something they can repeat when the next problem is unfamiliar.

Do not turn every error into a discussion about future exams. Focus on the next manageable step. If distress is persistent or wider concerns are present, discuss them with the school rather than treating tutoring as the answer to everything.
