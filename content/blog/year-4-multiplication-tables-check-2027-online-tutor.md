If your child is starting Year 4, this is a useful moment to notice how they find multiplication facts. Do they understand equal groups but need time to count? Do they recall some tables easily and lose track of others? An **online times tables tutor** should find out before asking for faster answers.

The government's [future primary assessment dates](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/primary-assessments-future-dates) place the 2027 multiplication tables check in the two week period beginning 7 June. This is England's Year 4 assessment, not a universal UK test. There is time to build confidence steadily rather than begin an autumn countdown.

## Understand what the check asks for

The [official parent information](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/multiplication-tables-check-information-for-parents/multiplication-tables-check-information-for-parents-text-version--2) describes an onscreen check of 25 questions, with six seconds to answer each one. It checks recall, so understanding multiplication and retrieving facts efficiently both matter.

A child who counts seven groups of eight correctly understands something important. They may still need help making that fact readily available. Treat the counting as a starting point rather than dismissing it as wrong.

Also separate knowledge from the practical act of entering an answer. When the learner knows a fact but takes time to type it, that calls for a different kind of practice from not knowing the multiplication itself.

## Begin with a small fact map

Try a short, untimed selection and record which facts are secure, which can be worked out and which are uncertain. Avoid checking every table in one sitting if the child becomes tired or frustrated.

Ask how they reached an answer. For eight times seven, a learner might use eight times five and add two more groups of eight. That reasoning gives a tutor something to strengthen. It can later support recall rather than remain a long calculation every time.

Work with a few uncertain facts alongside known ones. An entire session of difficult questions can make the child feel that they know nothing, even when most of their tables are secure.

## Connect the facts before practising speed

Use arrays, equal groups or a familiar context to make the operation visible. An array with four rows of six helps explain why four times six and six times four have the same total.

Related facts can make learning less isolated. If five times eight is forty, six times eight is one more group of eight. Ask the child to explain that relationship and then try a fresh example.

Include division occasionally to check meaning. If six groups of eight make forty eight, how many groups of eight are in forty eight? This is useful Maths learning beyond the particular check format.

## Use short retrieval sessions

Once the meaning is clear, mix a handful of facts and revisit them on different days. Keep a record of the facts that still need thought. Gradually introduce the pace of the check when accuracy and confidence are stable enough.

Do not make every practice round timed. A wrong timed answer needs a calm explanation afterwards. Repeating a rapid quiz without repairing the misconception can simply practise the same mistake again.

Praise a specific change: the child remembered a previously uncertain fact, used a known fact to check another, or entered answers more comfortably. Those observations are more useful than comparing their speed with a sibling.

## What an online Year 4 lesson should achieve

The lesson should leave the learner with a small target and the parent with a manageable practice idea. We would expect to see independent recall improve over several sessions and the child use multiplication in ordinary problems too.

Tell the tutor about any support your child receives at school and follow the school's advice about assessment arrangements. There is no need to predict the school's decisions based on an online practice result.
