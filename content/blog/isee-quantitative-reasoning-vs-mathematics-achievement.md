ISEE Quantitative Reasoning and Mathematics Achievement both involve mathematics, but preparing for them should go beyond repeating school exercises. Families often notice that a student calculates accurately yet struggles to choose a method when the question looks unfamiliar. That is a useful difficulty to investigate rather than a reason to assume the learner is weak at Maths.

For Middle and Upper Level, the [official ISEE test instructions](https://www.admission.org/assessments/isee/instructions/in-person-online) list Quantitative Reasoning as 37 questions in 35 minutes and Mathematics Achievement as 47 questions in 40 minutes. Other levels differ. Confirm the applicant's level before using those timings to organise practice.

## Look at the decision the question requires

A calculation can be straightforward once the problem has been represented. The challenge may be deciding what the numbers describe or whether enough information has been provided. A tutor should ask the student to explain that decision, not simply provide a faster calculation.

In preparation, Quantitative Reasoning can involve interpreting relationships and comparing quantities, while Mathematics Achievement practice checks mathematical knowledge and its application. The skills overlap. Strong teaching connects them without treating the sections as interchangeable practice lists.

## Use a comparison to expose an assumption

Try this original teaching example. Quantity A is x squared and Quantity B is x, with x positive. Which is larger? Trying x = 2 makes A larger, but trying x = 0.5 makes B larger. At x = 1 they are equal. Positivity alone does not fix one relationship.

A student who tests only a whole number may select a conclusion too quickly. The useful correction is to consider different allowed values, including fractions and boundary cases. This example is our own illustration, not a reproduced ISEE question.

Afterwards, change the condition to x greater than 1 and ask how that affects the comparison. The student should explain why the earlier counterexample is no longer allowed. This develops careful use of constraints rather than an automatic habit of testing random numbers.

## Check achievement skills with clear working

Consider a different original task. A rectangle has area 84 square units and width 7 units. Its length is 12 units because 84 ÷ 7 = 12. The perimeter is then 2 × (12 + 7) = 38 units.

An answer of 12 to the perimeter question shows that a correct intermediate calculation can still answer the wrong task. Ask the learner to underline what is requested, label the diagram and check the final unit. These habits support interpretation as well as calculation.

Now change the question so the perimeter is given and one side is missing. The learner must decide which relationship applies. Alternating these problems checks whether the formula is understood instead of merely recalled from the previous exercise.

## Build efficiency from understanding

Speed can improve when the student identifies structure, estimates sensibly and avoids unnecessary steps. Rushing an unfamiliar question usually makes the reasoning harder to inspect. Establish an accurate method first, then practise selecting it within a short mixed set.

Calculator use is generally prohibited unless specifically approved as an accommodation. Check the provider's [current Quick Facts Guide](https://cdn.erblearn.org/www/20260801_E3n_ISEE_QuickFacts_DIGITAL.pdf) and the student's approved arrangements. Preparation should match the actual testing conditions, including any accommodation, rather than assume a device will be available.

## Review errors in a way that changes the next attempt

Use categories such as misread condition, missing knowledge, calculation error and inefficient approach. Then give each error a practical next action. For a misread condition, the action might be to identify allowable values before comparing. For missing knowledge, teach the underlying relationship before timing another attempt.

Retest with a fresh problem several days later. A student who remembers the answer to the original task has not necessarily retained the reasoning. Ask for an explanation and a check as well as a final choice.
