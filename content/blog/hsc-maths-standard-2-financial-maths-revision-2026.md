This guide focuses on interest and depreciation. It is not a prediction of what will appear in the exam or a complete Standard 2 revision plan. Use it alongside your course materials, teacher feedback and personalised timetable.

## Identify what the percentage applies to

Two questions can contain the same amount, rate and number of years but require different models. Simple interest is calculated on the original principal. Compound interest changes the balance on which later interest is calculated. Depreciation reduces value rather than increasing it.

Before touching the calculator, write down the starting value, the rate per period and the number of periods. Underline wording about how often interest is compounded. An annual rate and monthly compounding need consistent treatment; they cannot be inserted as though every period were a year.

If you cannot explain the multiplier, pause there. Remembering a formula without understanding its inputs makes unfamiliar wording much harder.

## Work through one example with meaning

In this original practice example, $5,000 earns 4% interest per year, compounded annually for three years. There are no deposits, withdrawals or fees. Each year the balance is multiplied by 1.04.

The final balance is 5,000 × 1.04³ = $5,624.32. The interest earned is $624.32, found by subtracting the original $5,000. These are different answers. Read whether the question asks for the balance or the interest.

For comparison, simple interest under the same stated rate and duration would be 5,000 × 0.04 × 3 = $600. The compound result is larger because later interest is earned on a growing balance. That explanation is a useful check on your calculation.

## Change the model rather than copying the numbers

Now consider equipment worth $5,000 that loses 4% of its current value each year. The multiplier is 0.96. After three years its value is 5,000 × 0.96³ = $4,423.68.

Using 1.04 would increase the value, contradicting the question. Subtracting $200 three times would model a fixed annual loss based on the original value, which is different from losing a percentage of the current value.

Ask yourself what happens after the first year and whether the second year's change should be identical in dollars. This small explanation often reveals a misunderstanding that a page of calculator answers hides.

## Check calculator entries and rounding

Write the mathematical expression before entering it. Check that the exponent applies to the multiplier, and keep the full calculator value through intermediate steps. Round the final answer as requested, using cents when a monetary answer requires them.

Estimate first. A modest positive rate over three years should produce a balance somewhat above $5,000, not $50,000. A depreciating asset should finish below its starting value. Estimates will not replace working, but they can expose a misplaced decimal or incorrect multiplier.

If a question supplies a table, repayment schedule or other information, use those conditions. Do not force every financial problem into the example above.

## Make the remaining practice specific

Choose three financial questions from appropriate course resources. Attempt them without looking at solutions, then label each difficulty: model choice, period conversion, calculator entry or interpretation of the answer. Teach or revise the recurring step and try a fresh question afterwards.
