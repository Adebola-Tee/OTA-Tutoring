Probability questions seem manageable in the lesson, then become confusing when the wording changes. Later, inference questions introduce more symbols and procedures. Your child starts treating both topics as a collection of calculator commands.

It helps to separate the questions these topics answer. Probability uses a model to describe possible outcomes. Inference uses sample data to draw qualified conclusions about a population. They are connected, but knowing a button sequence for one does not establish understanding of the other.

## Find the group being counted

Consider a school with 100 students. Forty take music lessons, thirty play sport and twelve do both. The probability that a randomly chosen student takes music lessons is 40 out of 100.

Now ask for the probability that a student takes music lessons given that they play sport. The relevant group is the thirty students who play sport, and twelve of them take music. The conditional probability is therefore 12 out of 30, or 0.4.

The phrase “given that” changes the group under consideration. Ask your child to explain the denominator before calculating. If they cannot, drawing a two-way table can make the conditional group visible.

## Do not confuse independence with no overlap

In the example, taking music lessons has probability 0.4 overall and probability 0.4 among students who play sport. The counts are consistent with independence between those two categories in this particular dataset.

There are still twelve students in both groups. Independent events can overlap. Mutually exclusive events cannot occur together, which is a different idea. For events of positive probability, mutually exclusive events are not independent.

Have the learner compare examples rather than recite definitions. What changes when knowing one event has occurred changes the probability of another? That question reveals whether the relationship is understood.

## Understand the distribution before using it

Before applying a probability model, identify the random variable and check the model’s requirements. In a binomial setting, for example, the number of trials is fixed, each has two outcomes, the success probability is constant and the trials are independent.

Do not assume every question involving a count is binomial. Sampling without replacement can affect independence, and the context needs checking. A calculator will return an answer even when the selected model is unsuitable.

## Build an inference route

Start with what is being estimated or tested. Is it a population proportion or a mean? Which sample information is available? Which conditions must be examined before the chosen procedure is appropriate?

Then connect the numerical output to the claim. A confidence interval estimates a parameter with uncertainty. A hypothesis test assesses evidence against a specified null claim. Neither should finish with an unexplained decimal.

The [official AP Statistics course](https://apstudents.collegeboard.org/courses/ap-statistics) provides the current topic framework. Use materials aligned to the revised 2026–27 course, rather than assuming every chapter in an older workbook remains an exam priority.

## Fix the first weak decision

A useful short practice set might contain several questions about the same table, each with a different condition. Another might ask the learner to choose and justify an inference procedure without carrying out the calculations yet.
