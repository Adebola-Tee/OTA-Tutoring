If your child is struggling with **GCSE Chemistry**, opening the revision guide at page one and trying to relearn everything is probably not the best place to start.

Chemistry builds on itself.

If **atomic structure, bonding or chemical equations** are shaky, later topics such as electrolysis, quantitative chemistry and rates of reaction can suddenly feel impossible.

So before buying another revision book or adding more study hours, find the point where your child started getting lost.

## Start with these three Chemistry foundations

For AQA GCSE Chemistry, the first three areas are:

* Atomic structure and the periodic table
* Bonding, structure and properties of matter
* Quantitative chemistry

AQA describes these as **fundamental concepts that underpin much of the later Chemistry course**. [See the official AQA GCSE Chemistry specification](https://www.aqa.org.uk/subjects/chemistry/gcse/chemistry-8462/specification/subject-content).

That makes them a very useful starting point.

Ask your child some simple questions.

Can they explain the difference between an **atom, element, compound and ion**?

Can they explain **ionic, covalent and metallic bonding** rather than just recognise the words?

Can they balance a chemical equation?

Can they use relative formula mass and work through basic Chemistry calculations?

If several of those answers are no, you may have found the real problem.

## Chemistry often becomes difficult because students memorise without understanding

Chemistry contains a lot of terminology.

Students can easily fall into the habit of memorising:

**ionic bonding = transfer of electrons**

without really understanding what is being transferred, between which particles, and why ions with particular charges are produced.

That works until the exam asks them to apply the idea differently.

Then the memorised sentence is not enough.

Your child should be able to explain a concept in their own words and then use it in an unfamiliar question.

That is a much stronger test of understanding than asking:

**“Have you revised bonding?”**

## Check whether the real problem is Maths

Chemistry contains more calculation than some students expect.

A child may understand the scientific idea but lose marks through:

* ratios
* percentages
* rearranging equations
* standard form
* significant figures
* relative formula mass
* moles and concentrations

Quantitative Chemistry, in particular, relies on students connecting chemical equations with numerical relationships.

So look closely at recent papers.

If your child gets explanation questions right but calculations wrong, they may not need the whole Chemistry topic retaught.

They may need the **Maths underneath it strengthened**.

## Find out whether they are doing Combined or Separate Chemistry

Parents should confirm this early.

Is your child taking:

**Combined Science**

or

**Separate/Triple Science with GCSE Chemistry as its own qualification?**

There is overlap, but the content is not identical. AQA specifically identifies Chemistry-only material within the separate GCSE Chemistry course.

Also confirm the exam board.

AQA, Edexcel and OCR students should ultimately practise with materials that match the qualification they are actually sitting.

## Stop revising topics they already know

Take a recent Chemistry paper and divide the mistakes into three groups:

**Didn’t know it**

The content needs teaching.

**Knew it but couldn’t apply it**

The student needs more exam-style questions.

**Knew it but made a careless mistake**

The issue may be reading, calculations or exam technique.

That simple exercise is far more useful than another evening spent rereading the entire textbook.

## Do not leave required practicals until the end

Chemistry practicals are not something students can simply forget once the experiment has finished.

For AQA GCSE Chemistry, students complete **eight required practical activities**, while practical understanding and scientific skills can be assessed in the written examinations.

Your child should understand things such as:

* variables
* methods
* observations
* analysing results
* sources of error
* improvements
* interpreting graphs and data

If they have memorised a practical method but cannot explain **why each step is done**, that needs attention.

## What should you fix first?

Do not attack ten topics at once.

Start with the **three areas costing the most marks**.

For example:

**Week 1:** atomic structure and periodic table

**Week 2:** bonding and structure

**Week 3:** equations and quantitative Chemistry

Then begin mixing those areas into GCSE-style questions while moving on to later topics.

The goal is not to finish the syllabus fastest.

It is to stop building new Chemistry on top of foundations your child never properly understood.

## When might a Chemistry tutor help?

Extra support may be useful when your child:

* repeatedly struggles with the same concepts
* understands lessons but cannot answer exam questions
* has weak Chemistry calculations
* is falling behind before mocks
* has stopped knowing what to revise
* is losing confidence in Science

A good tutor should not simply start teaching every chapter.

They should look at the student’s actual work and ask:

**Where did understanding break down?**

That is where the teaching should begin.
