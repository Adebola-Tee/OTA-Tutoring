GCSE Maths Advice

My Child Understands Maths at Home but Fails Tests — Why?

By Taiwo Oloyede20 September 20265 min read

Your child sits at home, completes the Maths work, explains the method correctly — and then gets a disappointing test result.

It can be incredibly confusing.

You start wondering:

“But they know this. So what is going wrong?”

The answer is often that understanding Maths and performing well in a test are not exactly the same skill.

Here are the most common reasons.

1. They understand with support — but not independently

At home, your child may have:

a parent nearby notes open examples to look at plenty of time * someone prompting them when they get stuck

That can create the feeling that the topic is fully understood.

But in a test, all of that disappears.

The real question becomes:

Can they recognise the method and complete the question without any help?

A simple way to check is to give them a similar question the next day with no notes and no hints.

If they cannot start it independently, the knowledge is not secure yet.

2. They know the topic but do not recognise it in exam questions

This happens a lot in GCSE Maths.

A child may know how to solve percentages when the worksheet says:

Percentage Questions

But then struggle when the exact same skill appears inside a word problem about prices, profit or population.

That means the issue is not always knowledge.

It may be application.

GCSE questions often test whether students can recognise which method is needed without being told.

That skill has to be practised.

3. They rush when they feel under pressure

Some students completely change their behaviour in a test.

At home they work carefully.

In an exam, they:

skip steps misread questions make simple arithmetic mistakes forget negative signs copy numbers incorrectly choose the first method that comes to mind

Then, when you show them the question afterwards, they immediately say:

“Oh! I knew that.”

When this happens repeatedly, your child may need more timed exam practice, not more teaching of the topic itself.

4. They struggle to remember methods under test conditions

Understanding something during a lesson does not always mean it has moved into long-term memory.

A student may follow an explanation perfectly on Tuesday and forget half the method by Friday.

That is why GCSE Maths revision needs repetition.

A strong pattern is:

learn it → practise it → revisit it later → use it inside mixed questions

If your child only practises a topic once and then moves on, the knowledge may disappear before the next assessment.

5. One difficult question knocks their confidence

Tests are emotional as well as academic.

Some students hit one difficult question and immediately think:

“I can't do this paper.”

Then panic starts affecting the questions they normally could answer.

This is why exam technique includes knowing when to move on.

One difficult question should not be allowed to ruin the next ten.

6. Their practice at home may be too easy

This is an uncomfortable one, but it matters.

If your child practises only questions they already know how to do, home revision can create false confidence.

They need a mixture of:

familiar questions mixed-topic questions unfamiliar word problems timed questions * past-paper questions

A student should occasionally have to stop and think.

That is where real exam readiness develops.

7. The problem may be exam technique, not Maths ability

If your child regularly understands topics but scores poorly in tests, look carefully at the paper.

Are the marks being lost because of:

knowledge gaps?

Or because of:

timing, careless errors, poor interpretation and weak question strategy?

Those are very different problems.

What should parents do next?

Take one recent Maths test and go through every lost mark.

Label each mistake:

Didn't know the topic

Knew it but forgot the method

Misread the question

Careless calculation

Ran out of time

Panicked / left blank

You may be surprised by what you find.

If most errors come from only one or two categories, you now know exactly what needs attention.

Do not keep telling them to “revise more”

Sometimes the child is already revising.

The problem is that they are practising in a way that does not match the test.

More of the same revision may simply produce the same result.
