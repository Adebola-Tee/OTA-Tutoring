Maths enrichment

Does a Child Who Is Ahead in Maths Need a Tutor?

By Taiwo Oloyede9 October 20264 min read

Your child finishes the worksheet before everyone else and complains that Maths is boring. Extra pages of the same questions may keep them occupied, but they do not necessarily offer a worthwhile challenge.

A tutor can help an advanced learner when lessons provide depth, discussion and unfamiliar problems. Moving through next year's textbook is only one possibility, and it is not automatically the best one. First establish what your child understands and what kind of challenge is missing.

Fast answers are only part of the picture

Ask your child to explain a familiar method, find another route or create a question with a given answer. These tasks can reveal depth that a quick calculation does not show.

For example, ask for two different rectangles with area 24 square units. Then compare their perimeters. A 4 by 6 rectangle has perimeter 20; a 3 by 8 rectangle has perimeter 22. Equal area does not imply equal perimeter.

Now ask which whole-number rectangle with area 24 has the smallest perimeter. The child must organise possibilities and explain the comparison, rather than simply calculate one answer.

Offer problems that allow investigation

A useful challenge can begin with simple mathematics and develop through reasoning. How many different totals can be made using two numbered cards? What changes if order matters? Can the child predict a pattern and justify it?

Avoid presenting difficulty as a contest to prove how clever the learner is. A genuinely unfamiliar problem may require false starts. Make room for those attempts and discuss what each one reveals.

A learner who has rarely struggled may need help persisting when an answer is not immediate. The tutor should model trying, checking and revising, without turning every challenging task into a performance judgement.

Build connections before accelerating

Being fluent with procedures does not mean every concept is secure. A child may calculate fractions correctly but give a weak explanation of why the method works. Another may solve equations but struggle to model a real situation.

Deepening those connections can be worthwhile enrichment. Ask for a diagram, a counterexample or an explanation of when a rule does not apply. This develops mathematical flexibility without merely making the arithmetic larger.

The national curriculum mathematics programme includes fluency, reasoning and problem solving. For UK pupils, that provides a useful reminder that progress is broader than getting ahead in a sequence of written methods.

Keep the school involved

Ask the teacher what enrichment is already available and whether your child is secure across topics, not just in their favourites. Classroom boredom can have more than one cause, so do not assume a higher year-group textbook will solve it.

If acceleration is being considered, discuss curriculum continuity and the school's approach. A tutor should complement the learner's programme rather than create a disconnected race through content.
