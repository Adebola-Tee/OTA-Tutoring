HSC Advice

HSC 2026 Preparation After Trial Exams

By Taiwo Oloyede15 August 202611 min read

Finishing your HSC Trial exams can feel like reaching the end of something enormous. You have spent weeks revising, sitting papers and waiting for the pressure to ease.

Then you look at the calendar.

The actual HSC exams are still ahead.

For Year 12 students across New South Wales, the weeks immediately after the HSC Trials are some of the most important weeks of the entire school year. The 2026 HSC written examinations begin on Tuesday 13 October, which means students coming out of their Trial exams in August have roughly eight weeks to turn what they learned from the Trials into stronger final results.

That time can make a significant difference.

The aim now is not to restart the entire Year 12 syllabus from page one. It is to work out where marks were lost, fix those areas and gradually move towards timed HSC-style exam practice.

Your HSC Trial result is feedback, not your final result

One disappointing Trial mark can make students panic.

It should not.

HSC Trial exams are useful because they expose problems while there is still time to correct them. A Mathematics paper might show that your algebra is strong but probability is costing you marks. An English paper might reveal that you know your texts well but struggle to finish essays within the allocated time.

Instead of looking only at the final percentage, go through each paper and ask:

  • Which topics cost me the most marks?
  • Did I lose marks because I did not know the content?
  • Did I understand the question properly?
  • Did I run out of time?
  • Were there careless calculation errors?
  • Did I know the content but struggle to explain it clearly?
  • Are the same mistakes appearing repeatedly?

Your answers should determine what you study next.

A student who scored poorly because of weak knowledge needs a different revision plan from someone who knew the material but repeatedly ran out of time.

Week 1 after HSC Trials should be about finding the gaps

Do not immediately fill your timetable with five-hour study sessions.

Start with diagnosis.

Collect your Trial papers, teacher feedback, class assessments and any practice papers you completed before the Trials.

Divide each subject into three categories:

Strong

Topics you can answer accurately and confidently.

Needs practice

Topics you understand but cannot yet answer consistently under exam conditions.

Weak

Topics you regularly avoid, misunderstand or lose significant marks on.

Your weak list becomes your priority list for the next few weeks.

This is also the point where extra academic support can be particularly useful. At this stage of Year 12, students do not need another generic revision checklist. They need to know exactly what is preventing them from gaining marks.

Weeks 2 and 3 should rebuild weak topics

Once you know where the problems are, fix them before attempting endless full papers.

Suppose your Mathematics Trial showed weaknesses in functions, calculus or probability. Spending the next two weeks completing full Mathematics papers without correcting those gaps simply gives you more opportunities to make the same mistakes.

Instead:

  • Review the concept.
  • Complete several straightforward questions.
  • Move to harder HSC-style questions.
  • Mark the work carefully.
  • Record every mistake.
  • Return to the same skill several days later without looking at your previous solution.

The same principle applies outside Mathematics.

For English, you may need to improve essay structure, textual evidence, interpretation or your ability to adapt an argument to an unfamiliar question.

For Biology, Chemistry or Physics, the problem might be applying knowledge to unfamiliar scenarios rather than memorising content.

For Business Studies, Economics, Legal Studies or other writing-heavy subjects, students may know the syllabus but lose marks because their responses are too general.

Find the problem before choosing the solution.

Create an HSC mistake book

One of the simplest additions to an HSC study plan is also one of the most useful.

Keep one notebook or digital document purely for mistakes.

Every time you complete an HSC past paper, practice question or revision task and make an important error, record:

  • The question or topic
  • What you did
  • Why it was wrong
  • What you should have done
  • How you will recognise this type of question next time

This is much more useful than simply seeing a red cross, checking the answer and moving on.

As October approaches, your mistake book becomes a personalised revision guide containing the exact things most likely to cost you marks.

Weeks 4 and 5 should introduce serious past-paper practice

Once major gaps have been addressed, HSC past papers should become a bigger part of your preparation.

But there is a difference between doing past papers and learning from them.

After completing a paper, spend time analysing it.

Ask yourself:

  • Where were marks lost?
  • Which questions took too long?
  • Which topics appeared unexpectedly difficult?
  • Which command words caused problems?
  • Did you leave questions unfinished?
  • Could your written answers have been more precise?

Your mark is only one part of the exercise.

The real value is finding out what needs to change before the next paper.

For example, if you repeatedly spend too long on the first half of an exam and rush the final questions, the problem is no longer purely academic knowledge. It is exam management.

That should become part of your preparation too.

Start practising under HSC exam conditions

Studying a topic comfortably at home and performing during a formal examination are different skills.

As the HSC gets closer, some revision sessions should begin to resemble the real exam.

Set a timer.

Put your phone away.

Use only the materials permitted in the examination.

Sit somewhere quiet.

Complete the paper without checking notes halfway through.

Practising this way reveals things ordinary revision does not.

You discover how quickly your concentration drops, which sections consume too much time and how your accuracy changes when there is pressure.

That information allows you to adjust before October rather than discovering the problem during the actual HSC.

Weeks 6 and 7 should look increasingly like the real exam period

By this point, revision should shift.

You should still repair weaknesses, but full or substantial timed papers should become increasingly common.

A simple weekly structure might look like this:

  • Monday: Review one weak topic and complete targeted questions.
  • Tuesday: Complete a timed examination section or extended-response practice.
  • Wednesday: Review a second weak topic and correct Tuesday's work.
  • Thursday: Complete HSC past-paper questions under timed conditions.
  • Friday: Use lighter revision such as flashcards, formulas or essay plans.
  • Saturday: Complete a full or extended practice paper.
  • Sunday: Mark the paper, update your mistake book and plan the following week.

This does not need to be followed exactly.

Your school commitments, subjects and current performance should determine the amount of study required.

A realistic timetable that you follow consistently is far more useful than an impressive timetable you abandon after four days.

The final week is not the time to learn everything again

By the last week before the first HSC written examination, the goal should be control rather than panic.

Review important formulas.

Revisit your mistake book.

Practise difficult question types.

Review essay plans and key evidence.

Complete selected exam questions.

Check your examination timetable.

Prepare the equipment you will need.

Protect your sleep.

Avoid turning the final few nights into an attempt to relearn an entire subject.

The work done consistently throughout August and September matters far more than a desperate all-night revision session in October.

What if your HSC Trial marks were much lower than expected?

This is where students often make one of two mistakes.

Some panic and try to study everything at once.

Others assume the Trial result predicts their final HSC result and lose motivation.

Neither response helps.

A low Trial mark should trigger investigation.

For every subject where you performed below expectation, identify the biggest two or three reasons.

For example:

Mathematics

  • Weak algebra skills
  • Difficulty interpreting unfamiliar questions
  • Too many careless errors

English

  • Essays not answering the specific question
  • Weak textual evidence
  • Poor time management

Chemistry

  • Gaps in calculations
  • Difficulty applying theory
  • Weak extended responses

Now you have something actionable.

“Get better at Chemistry” is too vague.

“Improve equilibrium calculations and complete ten HSC-style questions on the topic this week” is something you can actually do.

Parents can help without turning every evening into an HSC conversation

Parents understandably become anxious when Trial results arrive.

But constantly asking a Year 12 student how much they studied that day can quickly create more tension than progress.

A better conversation is:

What did the Trial show that you need to improve?

From there, work out whether the student needs more practice, better resources, teacher feedback, help organising revision or additional tutoring.

Some students are perfectly capable of correcting weaknesses independently.

Others know they are struggling but cannot work out why.

That is usually when personalised support becomes particularly valuable.

There is little benefit in waiting until the week before the HSC to address a weakness that is already visible in August.

Do not measure your HSC preparation by hours alone

“I studied for six hours today” sounds impressive.

But what happened during those six hours?

A student who spends 90 focused minutes correcting recurring calculus mistakes may achieve more than someone who sits at a desk for five hours rereading highlighted notes.

A better way to measure revision is by outcomes.

Instead of:

Study Mathematics for two hours

try:

Complete and mark 15 integration questions and redo every incorrect question without notes.

Instead of:

Revise English

try:

Plan three responses to unseen essay questions and write one introduction under timed conditions.

Specific tasks make it much easier to see whether your HSC preparation is actually progressing.

Use active revision instead of repeatedly reading notes

Reading notes can make material feel familiar without proving that you can recall or apply it.

HSC preparation should increasingly involve doing something with the information.

Depending on the subject, that could mean:

  • Answering questions without notes
  • Completing HSC past-paper questions
  • Teaching a concept aloud
  • Writing essay plans from memory
  • Using flashcards for retrieval practice
  • Completing calculations
  • Explaining why an answer is correct
  • Comparing your response with marking criteria
  • Returning to previously incorrect questions

The closer you get to the HSC, the more your study should resemble the skills you will actually need in the examination.

How many past papers should you complete before the HSC?

There is no magic number.

Ten poorly reviewed papers are less useful than five papers that have been carefully completed, marked, analysed and corrected.

Past papers should show improvement.

If you scored 55% on one paper, investigate why.

Then work on those areas before doing the next comparable paper.

The goal is not to collect completed papers.

The goal is to make fewer mistakes every time.

Your August HSC preparation matters more than it feels

October may still look comfortably far away.

It is not.

The 2026 HSC written examination period begins on 13 October, and the weeks between the Trial exams and that first written paper will pass quickly.

But there is enough time to make meaningful progress.

Students can strengthen weak topics.

Essay writing can improve.

Mathematical errors can be corrected.

Exam timing can become much better.

Past-paper scores can rise.

The students who benefit most from this period are not necessarily the ones studying every available hour. They are the ones paying attention to what their Trial exams revealed and using that information intelligently.

So before creating another enormous revision timetable, open your Trial papers.

Find the marks you lost.

Find out why you lost them.

Then make the next eight weeks about getting those marks back.
