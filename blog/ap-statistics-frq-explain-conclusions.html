AP Statistics FRQ help

How to Explain Conclusions in AP Statistics FRQs

By Taiwo Oloyede6 October 20264 min read

The working is there. The calculator output is correct. But the final sentence says only “reject” or “the answer is significant.” For an AP Statistics free response question, that can leave the actual statistical conclusion unfinished.

Students need to explain what the analysis says about a particular question. That means identifying the population, the variable or parameter, and the strength of the evidence. It also means avoiding claims that the study design cannot justify.

Return to the original claim

Before writing a conclusion, read the investigative question again. What was being compared or estimated? Which population does the question concern? Those details should guide the final sentence.

Suppose a study asks whether the proportion of students who cycle to school exceeds 30%. A relevant conclusion concerns that population proportion. It does not concern the average cycling distance, the particular students’ future behaviour or whether cycling improves health.

Ask the learner to underline the population and parameter in the question. Then have them say the conclusion aloud before writing it. This often reveals whether the statistical symbols have remained connected to the context.

Interpret a confidence interval carefully

Imagine an appropriate procedure produces a 95% confidence interval from 0.52 to 0.64 for a population proportion. A useful interpretation identifies the population and states that we are 95% confident its true proportion lies between those bounds.

It does not mean 95% of individuals have a characteristic between 52% and 64%. It also does not mean 95% of the sample lies within the interval. The interval estimates a population parameter, not the spread of individual observations.

Discuss what repeated use of the method means: in repeated random sampling under the relevant conditions, about 95% of intervals constructed this way would contain the true parameter. This helps explain the confidence language without treating the fixed parameter as a random sample result.

Distinguish evidence from proof

A small p-value indicates that the observed result, or something more extreme in the relevant direction, would be unlikely under the null hypothesis. It is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true.

If the result does not meet the chosen significance threshold, avoid writing that the null has been proved. The conclusion is that there is insufficient evidence for the alternative claim at that threshold. Weak evidence and proof of no effect are different statements.

Use the exact hypotheses and conditions of the problem when practising. A memorised sentence that reverses the claim can turn an accurate calculation into an incorrect conclusion.

Keep study limitations in view

Even a statistically significant result does not automatically establish causation. Random sampling and random assignment affect which conclusions are supported. Practical importance is another question; a small difference can be statistically detectable without being especially useful.

The current AP Statistics assessment information should guide exam preparation. The course and exam changed for 2026–27, so use current examples and teacher feedback when checking written responses.

Practise repairing one sentence

Take a marked answer and identify the missing detail. Was the population unnamed? Was the parameter confused with an individual observation? Did the student overstate the evidence? Rewrite that sentence, then try a fresh problem with a different context.
