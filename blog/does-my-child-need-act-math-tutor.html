Does My Child Need an ACT Math Tutor?

By Taiwo Oloyede9 October 20264 min read

Your child opens the answer explanation, nods, and says they understand. A week later, a similar ACT Math question goes wrong again. That pattern is more informative than the number of hours spent revising. It suggests that seeing a solution has not yet become being able to build one.

An ACT Maths tutor may help when practice repeatedly exposes a difficulty the learner cannot resolve alone. It is not a requirement for every student, and a disappointing score by itself does not identify the right support. Look at the work behind the result before choosing lessons.

Can your child explain why the method works?

Take one missed question and ask for the reasoning, not a recital of steps. Suppose a price rises from $40 to $50. The increase is $10, and the percentage increase is 10 divided by 40, multiplied by 100. The starting price is the comparison base.

A student who divides by 50 may understand the arithmetic but not the meaning of percentage change. Another student may choose the correct denominator but struggle with division. Those learners need different teaching. A tutor should investigate this before assigning a large mixed worksheet.

If your child can identify the misconception, use a reliable explanation and successfully solve fresh questions afterwards, independent preparation may already be working. Give that progress room to develop.

Watch for dependence on familiar questions

Some students appear accurate because they have repeated the same practice set. Ask how they manage when the wording changes or the unknown appears in a different position. For a rectangle, finding area from length and width is different from finding width when area and length are given.

Help becomes more valuable when the student needs someone to connect those versions. Look for transfer to unfamiliar questions rather than counting how many completed pages sit in a folder.

The same applies to calculator use. Fast button pressing does not show whether the student has entered the correct expression or interpreted the result correctly. Written setup and an approximate expected answer should still be visible.

Is preparation becoming difficult to organise?

A learner juggling school assessments and admissions tests may not know which weaknesses deserve attention first. Repeatedly switching between apps, videos and full papers can consume time without producing a coherent plan.

Our article on an ACT Math score that stops improving explains how to turn a practice result into a small number of priorities. A tutor can help organise that sequence, but should be able to explain why those priorities were chosen.

Ask about the intended test date and what the learner can realistically practise between lessons. More sessions are not automatically better if the student has no time to use the feedback.

Choose teaching rather than answer delivery

Before booking, ask how the tutor sees the student's work, checks understanding and decides when to revisit a skill. Online lessons should involve the student attempting questions, explaining choices and responding to feedback.

Check that preparation reflects the current test. The official ACT Math description sets out the mathematical areas being assessed. A provider should distinguish ACT preparation from a generic SAT programme and from ordinary school homework support.

Be cautious about guaranteed score jumps. A useful teacher can describe a plan and review progress; they cannot know the final result before seeing the learner work.
