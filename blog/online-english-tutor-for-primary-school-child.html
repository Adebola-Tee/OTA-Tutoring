Online English Tutor for a Primary School Child: What Should Lessons Include?

By Taiwo Oloyede26 September 20263 min read

If you search for an online English tutor for my child, you may find hundreds of profiles. The useful question is what happens in the actual lesson. A child who reads fluently but cannot explain a text needs different support from a child who has ideas but struggles to write a paragraph.

Start with the skill, not just the year group

Bring one piece of schoolwork, a recent reading passage and a writing sample. Ask the tutor to notice where the child succeeds independently and where they need a prompt. For younger readers, that might be decoding or reading accuracy. For an older primary pupil, it might be vocabulary, inference, sentence construction or organising ideas.

England's primary English curriculum covers reading, writing and spoken language. Your child's school or international curriculum may differ, so share the actual programme rather than assuming every English lesson follows the same sequence.

What a useful online lesson can look like

  • Read together: Let the child read a short text and explain what happened in their own words.
  • Talk about evidence: Ask, “Which word made you think that?” before giving an interpretation.
  • Write something short: Turn one spoken idea into two or three clear sentences.
  • Give specific feedback: Choose one improvement, such as adding evidence or changing a vague verb.
  • Try again independently: Finish with a fresh sentence or question to see whether the teaching transferred.

A tutor should share the screen or annotate a passage while the child actively responds. Watching an adult talk for an hour is a poor substitute for practising English.

Questions to ask before you book

Ask how the tutor will check reading comprehension, how writing will be reviewed between lessons, and whether the child will receive a small amount of targeted practice. Ask what progress would look like after a month. A realistic answer might be “they explain answers using evidence more often,” rather than a guaranteed grade.

If your child enjoys stories but freezes when asked to write, say so. If they speak confidently but struggle to read unfamiliar words, say that too. The starting point changes the lesson.
