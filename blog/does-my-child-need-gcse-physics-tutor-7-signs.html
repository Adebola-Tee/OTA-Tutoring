GCSE Physics Advice

Does My Child Need a GCSE Physics Tutor? 7 Signs Parents Should Notice

By Taiwo Oloyede21 September 20265 min read

Not every student who finds Physics difficult needs a tutor.

Sometimes a topic is simply hard at first.

Sometimes one poor test is just one poor test.

But there is a point where “we’ll give it time” turns into months of the same mistakes.

If your child is preparing for GCSE Physics, these are seven signs that extra support may be worth considering.

1. Their Physics grade has stopped improving

Your child may be revising, completing homework and attending lessons — but their test scores stay almost identical.

That usually means effort is not the main problem.

Something specific is not being fixed.

Perhaps they keep losing marks on calculations.

Perhaps written explanations are too vague.

Perhaps they understand topics in class but cannot apply them independently.

When the same grade appears again and again, it is worth looking deeper.

2. They know the formula but still cannot answer the question

This is one of the clearest Physics warning signs.

Your child may know an equation perfectly, yet struggle when the question is presented in an unfamiliar way.

They may not know:

which equation to choose how to rearrange it whether units need converting what values should be substituted

That is not simply an equation-memory problem.

It is an application problem.

Our guide on why students know GCSE Physics equations but still lose marks explains this in more detail.

3. They keep saying, “I understand it when the teacher explains it”

That sentence matters.

Following an explanation is not the same as being able to solve a question independently.

A simple check is to give your child a fresh exam-style question after the lesson.

No notes.

No hints.

If they cannot decide how to begin, the topic may not be secure yet.

A tutor can help bridge that gap between:

“I understand what you did”

and

“I can do this myself.”

4. Physics calculations are dragging everything down

Physics contains a lot of Maths.

Students may need to rearrange formulas, use standard form, convert units, interpret graphs and work with significant figures.

So sometimes a child who appears weak in Physics actually has a Maths weakness sitting underneath it.

Watch for repeated problems with:

rearranging equations calculator use unit conversions standard form graphs ratios

If those areas keep appearing in the mistakes, simply rereading the Physics textbook will not solve the problem.

5. They avoid long-answer and practical questions

Some students are comfortable with short recall questions but panic when they see:

“Explain…”

“Describe…”

“Evaluate…”

or a required-practical question.

They may know the Science but not know how to turn it into an answer that earns marks.

That becomes increasingly important as students move into GCSE-style assessments.

A tutor can help them practise how to use precise scientific language, interpret data and build answers that match what the question is asking.

6. Their confidence in Physics is disappearing

Listen to what your child says.

“Physics makes no sense.”

“I’m going to fail anyway.”

“I hate calculation questions.”

Those comments often appear after repeated difficulty.

Once students expect to get Physics wrong, they may stop attempting difficult questions properly.

Support becomes especially useful when the goal is not only to teach content but to help the student start experiencing success again.

If mocks are approaching and Physics has become a major concern, our guide for parents whose child is struggling with GCSE Physics before mocks may help you decide what to prioritise first.

7. They are working hard, but the mistakes never change

This is probably the biggest sign.

A student can spend hours revising and still make exactly the same mistakes every week.

That usually means their revision is not targeted enough.

They may be:

rereading notes watching videos doing easy questions avoiding weak topics * taking past papers without properly reviewing them

More revision does not automatically mean better revision.

Sometimes the student needs someone to look at their work and say:

“This is the pattern. This is where the marks are going. This is what we fix first.”

What should a good GCSE Physics tutor actually do?

A tutor should not simply start from Chapter 1 and march through the textbook.

They should first find out:

which exam board your child is taking whether they are doing Combined or Separate Science their current and target grades which topics are weakest * whether the problem is content, Maths, practicals or exam technique

Then the lessons should be built around that.

That is what makes tutoring different from simply adding another Physics class.

So, does your child need a tutor?

A tutor may be worth considering when your child is no longer closing the gap alone.

That does not mean they are incapable.

It simply means the current support is not yet solving the problem.

And the earlier that becomes clear, the more time there is to fix it before the real GCSE exams.
