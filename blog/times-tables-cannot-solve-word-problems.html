Why Times Tables Are Not Enough for Maths Word Problems

By Taiwo Oloyede9 October 20264 min read

Your child answers seven times eight almost instantly. Then a question about seven bags of eight marbles leaves them unsure which operation to use. The multiplication fact is available; recognising when it belongs in a story is a separate skill.

Times-table recall is useful, but it does not automatically teach mathematical relationships. Before asking for more speed practice, check whether the child understands what the groups represent. That connection gives remembered facts a purpose.

Start with a situation they can show

Put three small groups of four objects on a table. Ask how many groups there are, how many objects are in each group and how many there are altogether. Write 3 × 4 = 12 beside the model.

Now reverse the question. If twelve objects are shared equally among three people, each gets four. If twelve objects are packed into groups of four, there are three groups. Both use division, but the unknown has a different meaning.

A child who recites 12 divided by 3 equals 4 may still confuse those stories. Let them move the objects and describe the result before asking for an equation. The explanation matters as much as the total.

Avoid teaching a hunt for keywords

Words such as altogether do not always mean addition. Three boxes containing six pencils each have eighteen pencils altogether. Multiplication describes the equal groups efficiently.

Similarly, the word more may appear in a subtraction question. If Laila has nine stickers, which is three more than Sam has, Sam has six. Adding three to nine would reverse the relationship.

Ask the child to retell the story in a sentence. Who has what? What changes? What must be found? A simple drawing often clarifies the comparison better than highlighting a single word.

Use small numbers at first so calculation does not hide the reading difficulty. Once the relationship is understood, increase the numbers while keeping the structure familiar.

Pair a fact with several different stories

Take 6 × 5 = 30. It could describe six rows of five seats, six weeks of saving five coins, or a rectangle six units long and five units wide. Discuss what remains the same and what changes in each story.

Then include a near example that requires another operation. Six coins followed by five more coins is addition, even though the same numbers appear. The child needs to choose from the relationship, not from the numbers themselves.

For UK learners, the primary maths teaching guidance is a useful reference for how number understanding develops. For children following another curriculum, match examples to their school's expectations rather than assuming the same year-group sequence everywhere.

Give them a way to check the answer

Ask whether the result is reasonable. Seven bags of eight marbles should contain more than eight marbles. If the answer is fifteen, the child may have added the two numbers without modelling the groups.

Checking is easier when it is linked to the story. Dividing fifty-six marbles back into seven equal bags gives eight per bag. This confirms the relationship as well as the calculation.
