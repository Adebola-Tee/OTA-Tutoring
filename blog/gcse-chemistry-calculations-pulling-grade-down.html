GCSE Chemistry Advice

GCSE Chemistry Calculations Are Pulling My Child’s Grade Down — What Can Help?

By Taiwo Oloyede21 September 20266 min read

Your child understands bonding.

They can explain reactions.

They know the periodic table.

Then a GCSE Chemistry calculation appears and suddenly the marks disappear.

This is more common than parents realise.

Chemistry calculations combine two skills at once:

understanding the Chemistry + knowing what to do mathematically.

So when a student repeatedly loses calculation marks, the solution is not necessarily to revise the entire Chemistry course again.

First, find out which part of the calculation is breaking down.

Start with the calculation, not the final answer

Take a recent Chemistry paper and look at the working.

Did your child:

choose the wrong equation? calculate relative formula mass incorrectly? struggle with moles? fail to use the balanced equation? mix up cm³ and dm³? make a percentage error? struggle to rearrange a formula? lose the answer through standard form or significant figures?

AQA GCSE Chemistry specifically requires mathematical skills including decimals, standard form, ratios, fractions, percentages, significant figures, rearranging equations and substituting values with appropriate units.

That means one weak Maths skill can keep appearing across several Chemistry topics.

Relative formula mass needs to become automatic

If your child cannot confidently calculate relative formula mass (Mr), later quantitative Chemistry becomes much harder.

For example, they need to recognise that the small numbers in a chemical formula tell them how many atoms of each element are present.

This sounds basic.

But one incorrect Mr can ruin every calculation that follows.

Before moving deeper into moles, check that your child can calculate Mr accurately from unfamiliar chemical formulae.

Moles are where many students start getting lost

For Higher Tier students, moles connect:

mass → relative formula mass → amount of substance

Then balanced equations introduce ratios between different substances.

This is where students often memorise one formula but struggle when the question changes slightly.

AQA expects Higher Tier students to calculate moles from mass and relative formula mass, interpret balanced equations in terms of moles and use those relationships to calculate masses of reactants or products.

The best way to practise is not twenty identical mole questions.

Use different versions:

Find the moles.

Then:

Find the mass.

Then:

Use a balanced equation to find the amount of another substance.

If your child can only solve the first version, the skill is not secure yet.

Balanced equations are not just a separate topic

Students sometimes think:

“I know how to balance equations, so that topic is finished.”

It is not.

Balanced equations become part of calculation questions because the coefficients show the relationship between substances.

If your child ignores that ratio, the rest of the calculation can be perfectly executed and still produce the wrong answer.

This is why weak foundations in equations often show up later as “calculation problems”.

Our guide on what to do when your child is struggling with GCSE Chemistry explains why atomic structure, bonding and quantitative Chemistry are worth securing early.

Concentration questions need strong unit awareness

Concentration can look straightforward until different units appear.

Students may need to work with relationships involving:

mass volume concentration moles, depending on tier and course

One particularly common trap is volume.

A student may need to convert between cm³ and dm³ before calculating.

If they miss that conversion, the method can be correct but the answer can be wildly wrong.

Teach your child to pause before every calculation and ask:

“Are all of these values in the units I need?”

That five-second check can save a surprising number of marks.

Percentages appear in several different disguises

Chemistry may involve calculations such as:

percentage yield atom economy percentage composition percentage change

A student who is shaky with percentages can therefore lose marks in several parts of the course.

AQA’s quantitative Chemistry content includes percentage yield and atom economy alongside mass, mole and concentration calculations.

If percentages are the problem, it may be more efficient to strengthen that Maths skill properly rather than teaching each Chemistry calculation as though it were completely unrelated.

Encourage a consistent calculation routine

Students often lose marks because their working becomes chaotic.

A simple routine helps:

1. Write down what the question gives you.

2. Identify what you need to calculate.

3. Write the relationship or equation.

4. Check the units.

5. Substitute carefully.

6. Calculate.

7. Add the correct unit and check whether the answer is sensible.

That structure slows students down slightly at first.

Eventually, it makes them faster because they stop restarting calculations after avoidable mistakes.

Do not let the calculator hide weak understanding

A calculator can produce an answer.

It cannot decide whether the calculation makes sense.

Ask your child to explain:

Why are you dividing here?

Why are you using this ratio?

Why did you convert the volume?

What does this number actually represent?

If they cannot explain the step, they may be following a memorised procedure rather than understanding the Chemistry.

That becomes dangerous when the exam changes the wording.

What should parents practise first?

Do not tackle every Chemistry calculation at once.

Try this order:

First: relative formula mass and balanced equations

Then: moles and mass calculations

Then: calculations using equation ratios

Then: concentration and unit conversions

Then: percentage yield, atom economy and other percentage questions

The exact order may change depending on your child’s tier and exam board, but the principle stays the same:

build one calculation skill securely before stacking another one on top.

When does tutoring become useful?

Consider targeted help when your child:

understands Chemistry theory but repeatedly fails calculations cannot identify which method to use struggles with moles despite repeated revision has weak underlying Algebra or percentages gets the method right but keeps making unit errors is practising regularly but their calculation marks are not improving

At that point, another generic revision video may not be enough.

Someone needs to identify the repeated mistake and work directly on it.
