Primary Maths Tutoring

What happens in an online Year 5 Maths lesson

By Taiwo Oloyede15 October 20264 min read

When you look for an online Year 5 Maths tutor, you want to know more than which topics are available. Will your child explain their thinking, work through a difficulty and use the method without help? Those are useful questions whether school Maths feels difficult or your child is ready for a deeper challenge.

At OTA Learning Studio, we discuss the learner's schoolwork and goals before planning paid lessons through Google Meet. Our free parent consultation is a conversation about suitability, available times and fees. Here is an example of what purposeful Year 5 teaching could involve.

Choose a focus from the child's work

A worksheet with several wrong fraction answers does not tell the whole story. Ask the child to explain one attempt. They may understand equal parts but confuse the notation, or follow a procedure without understanding the size of the answer. We need to locate the difficulty before choosing practice.

Bring recent schoolwork and the teacher's current topic. England's Year 5 Maths programme provides a reference for the year, while individual schools choose their teaching sequence. British international school families should share that sequence too. This guide uses the English curriculum; our Australian Year 5 fractions guide serves a different curriculum context.

Make the fraction calculation visible

Suppose a recipe uses three quarters of a litre of juice and another one eighth of a litre is added. How much juice is there now? This original example lets us explore 3/4 + 1/8 with a labelled bar representing one litre.

Divide the bar into eight equal parts. Three quarters covers six of those eighths, so 3/4 is equivalent to 6/8. Adding one more eighth gives 7/8 of a litre. The pieces remain eighths; we have changed how many are counted, not the size of each piece.

Before writing the answer, estimate. Three quarters plus a small amount should be greater than three quarters and less than one litre. If a child adds the numerators and denominators to get 4/12, the estimate and bar help them question that result instead of merely memorising another rule.

Change the task when understanding is secure

A learner who can explain the first example needs something more thoughtful than a long page of identical sums. Ask how much more juice would fill the litre: one eighth. Then ask them to create a different addition with a total of seven eighths and justify it.

For another child, we might return to equivalent fractions with simpler drawings first. The point is to adjust the teaching to the answer and explanation we hear. Working through older content is useful when it repairs a prerequisite; racing ahead is unnecessary when a richer problem offers appropriate challenge.

Give the learner an active role online

We use shared material alongside the child's own paper and spoken explanations. Agree how written work will be shown before the lesson. A working microphone, clear camera view when needed and a quiet place help us follow the method, including changes and crossed out attempts.

Parents can help with the connection without supplying each step. When a child pauses, allow a little thinking time. Questions such as “What does this part represent?” are more revealing than telling them which number to write next. Let us know if a particular way of presenting work is difficult.

Check what carries over to another day

An independent question could ask for 1/2 + 3/8. Can the learner explain why one half becomes four eighths and obtain seven eighths? Revisiting the idea later shows more than completing a nearly identical question immediately after the explanation.

A short practice task can include a calculation, a diagram and an explanation of a mistake. We look for clearer reasoning and less prompting, then adjust the next priority. Parents should know what the practice is checking rather than receive pages without a purpose.
