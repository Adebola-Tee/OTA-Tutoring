ACT Science Advice

ACT Science: How Physics, Chemistry and Biology Questions Actually Test Reasoning

By Taiwo Oloyede23 September 20264 min read

The words Biology, Chemistry and Physics can make ACT Science look like three school courses compressed into one test. Students may assume they need to memorise every formula, process and scientific fact before they begin.

That misunderstanding can waste preparation time. The contexts vary, but the central task is usually the same: use the supplied evidence to make an accurate decision.

Biology questions often test relationships

A passage may involve plants, enzymes, populations or genetics. The student may need to compare treatment groups, identify a trend or decide whether results support a hypothesis.

The organism may be unfamiliar. That is deliberate. Students should identify the variables and evidence rather than panic because they have not studied that species.

Chemistry questions often test variables and patterns

Tables may show temperature, concentration, pH, mass or reaction time. Questions can ask students to locate values, compare trials or predict what happens when one condition changes.

Basic knowledge can help, but the answer must fit the presented experiment. A memorised rule should not override the actual data.

Physics questions often test graphs and proportional reasoning

Students may see motion, forces, waves, electricity or energy. They should inspect units, slope, direction and relationships before searching memory for a formula.

Sometimes a calculation is needed. Often the choices can be narrowed through trend, comparison or estimation.

The shared reasoning skills matter most

Across all three subjects, students repeatedly need to:

  • locate and compare data
  • identify variables and controls
  • understand how an experiment was designed
  • make predictions from a pattern
  • evaluate whether evidence supports a claim
  • compare competing explanations

ACT’s official Science test description similarly emphasises interpretation, analysis, evaluation, reasoning and problem-solving across the natural sciences.

What knowledge is still useful?

Students benefit from broad foundations: reading units, understanding variables, recognising common graph relationships and knowing basic concepts such as pH, density, inheritance and forces.

When a genuine background gap appears, repair it briefly and return to application. Avoid turning one missed question into days of general content revision.

A better way to practise

Mix passage contexts so the student cannot depend on a familiar subject heading. After each question, require three answers:

  1. What did the question ask?
  2. Where is the supporting evidence?
  3. What reasoning connects the evidence to the option?

Then classify any error as navigation, data reading, experimental reasoning, evaluation, timing or content. Patterns will emerge quickly.

If memorisation has become the main preparation method, read why more Biology and Chemistry revision may not improve ACT Science.

Parents can reassure students that unfamiliar science does not mean inaccessible science. Encourage them to translate the passage into variables, trends and claims. Once the surface topic is stripped away, many questions become manageable.

The goal is not to make every child an expert in three sciences before test day. It is to make them disciplined users of scientific evidence.

Build confidence through unfamiliarity

Include some practice passages on topics the student has never studied. Before answering, ask them to label the figures, variables and claims. This teaches an important lesson: unfamiliar vocabulary can be present while the reasoning remains accessible.

Afterwards, discuss which information was actually needed. Students often discover that a dense introduction contained far more detail than the questions required. Learning to tolerate that uncertainty prevents a strange topic from controlling the rest of the section.
