GCSE Physics Advice

My Child Is Good at Maths but Struggles With Physics — Why?

By Taiwo Oloyede21 September 20265 min read

It can be confusing.

Your child is strong at Maths.

They are comfortable with Algebra.

They can rearrange equations.

Their calculation skills are fine.

Yet somehow, GCSE Physics is still one of their weaker subjects.

Parents often assume that if a child is good at Maths, Physics should come naturally.

Sometimes it does.

But Physics asks for something extra:

your child has to understand what the Maths actually means in the real situation.

That is where many strong Maths students start to struggle.

Physics is not just Maths with formulas

A Maths question often tells the student quite clearly what kind of problem they are solving.

Physics may not.

A Physics question might describe:

a car slowing down a circuit a spring an electrical appliance a moving object an energy transfer

Your child has to work out which Physics idea applies before they even begin the calculation.

So they may be perfectly capable of solving:

V = IR

but still struggle to recognise when that is the right relationship to use.

That is not weak Maths.

It is weak Physics application.

They may understand the numbers but not the concept

Take electricity.

A strong Maths student can manipulate the equation for current, voltage and resistance easily.

But do they actually understand what resistance means?

Do they know what happens to current when resistance changes?

Can they explain why two components behave differently?

Physics exams often move between:

calculation → explanation → graph → practical → calculation again

A student who only feels comfortable when numbers appear can lose a lot of marks elsewhere.

Physics questions hide the method

This is a major difference.

A Maths worksheet may say:

Solve simultaneous equations.

The student instantly knows what skill is being tested.

A Physics paper may give a paragraph about two objects colliding and expect the student to realise that momentum is relevant.

That recognition has to be trained.

Your child needs practice asking:

What Physics topic is this actually testing?

What information have I been given?

What relationship connects it?

That thinking happens before any calculation starts.

Strong Maths students can rush Physics questions

This happens more than parents expect.

A child sees numbers and immediately starts calculating.

But Physics questions often include information that must first be interpreted.

If they rush, they may:

use the wrong equation calculate the wrong quantity ignore a unit conversion miss what the question actually asked * produce a mathematically correct answer to the wrong problem

If your child frequently knows the equations but still loses marks, our guide on why students know GCSE Physics formulas but still get calculations wrong explains this problem in more detail.

Written explanations may be the real weakness

Ask to see your child's latest Physics paper.

You may discover that calculation questions are actually fine.

The marks are disappearing on questions that begin with:

Explain

Describe

Compare

Evaluate

A strong Physics answer needs accurate scientific language.

For example, saying:

“The particles move more.”

may not be enough.

The examiner may need the student to explain what happens to kinetic energy, particle speed, collisions or forces, depending on the question.

Knowing the idea in your head is not enough.

The answer has to say it precisely.

Graphs can also catch strong Maths students out

Your child may be excellent at plotting and reading graphs in Maths.

Physics graphs add another layer.

They may need to explain what the gradient or area represents physically.

They must connect the shape of the graph back to the science.

That is the difference.

In Maths:

What is the gradient?

In Physics:

What does this gradient tell us about the motion?

Same mathematical skill.

Different thinking.

Required practicals may be costing marks too

GCSE Physics is not only equations and theory.

Students also need to understand experimental work.

They may be asked about:

independent and dependent variables control variables accuracy repeat readings graphs sources of error * how to improve a method

A child who loves calculations but ignores practical questions can easily end up with a grade that feels surprisingly low.

So what should they work on?

If the Maths is already strong, do not waste revision time endlessly practising straightforward calculations.

Focus instead on:

1. Identifying the topic hidden inside the question

2. Explaining Physics ideas in precise language

3. Practising unfamiliar exam-style questions

4. Interpreting graphs and data physically

5. Understanding required practicals

6. Reviewing why wrong answers were wrong

That is where the marks may actually be hiding.

Do not assume they need “more Maths”

This is the key point.

If your child is already confident with Algebra and calculations, extra Maths worksheets may not improve their Physics grade.

They may need help learning how to think like a Physics student.

That means connecting the calculation to the concept.

Connecting the graph to the situation.

Connecting the practical to the underlying Physics.
