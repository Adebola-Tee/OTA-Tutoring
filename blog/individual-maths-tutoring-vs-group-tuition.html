Should You Choose Individual or Group Maths Tuition?

By Taiwo Oloyede9 October 20264 min read

One parent recommends individual lessons because their child finally began asking questions. Another says a small group helped their child enjoy Maths again. Both experiences can be true. The right arrangement depends on what the learner needs to happen during the lesson.

Before comparing prices or package names, identify the main difficulty. Does your child need a different starting point, more time to explain a method, regular practice or discussion with other learners? Those needs help you judge the teaching arrangement more usefully.

When individual attention can matter

Consider a child who struggles with place value while classmates are moving through written subtraction. A lesson that follows the group's current worksheet may leave the foundational gap untouched. Individual teaching can spend time on that prerequisite and change direction when the learner's explanation reveals uncertainty.

It can also provide space for a hesitant student to speak, make mistakes and show unfinished working. That does not mean individual lessons automatically build confidence; the teacher still needs to ask useful questions and give the child opportunities to work independently.

Ask how the tutor will avoid dependence. A session in which the adult completes every first step may feel supportive but leave little change in what the child can do alone.

When a small group can be useful

Learners with compatible needs can compare methods and hear different explanations. One child might draw a fraction model while another uses a number line. Discussing why both show the same amount can deepen understanding.

The group needs to be matched thoughtfully. Similar ages do not guarantee similar starting knowledge. Ask what happens if one learner finishes quickly while another needs the underlying idea explained again.

The EEF's small group tuition review highlights the importance of targeting pupils' needs. Its findings concern studied educational interventions; they should not be treated as a guarantee for any commercial lesson package.

Compare feedback rather than labels

A small class can still leave a child passive. An individual session can still move too quickly. Watch whether the student attempts questions, explains decisions and receives feedback on their own working.

Ask practical questions before booking:

  • How many learners normally attend, and how are they matched?
  • Will the teacher see each child's written work during the lesson?
  • What happens when a prerequisite gap appears?
  • How will independent progress be reviewed?

An answer such as we cover the whole curriculum is not enough to explain how your child will be taught. Look for a clear response tied to their current difficulty.

Consider the timetable and home practice

The arrangement should fit the child's workload. A cheaper package is not better value if the student cannot attend consistently or the level is unsuitable. Equally, the most intensive package may be unnecessary for a small, specific gap.

Online delivery should allow clear communication and visible working. Ask what equipment is needed, whether practice is set and how the teacher uses it in later lessons. Parents should understand their role without being expected to reteach the entire session.
