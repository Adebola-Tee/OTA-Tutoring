IB Maths Course Choices

Is IB Maths AA or AI the Right Course for Your Child?

By Taiwo Oloyede6 October 20264 min read

Your child chose IB Maths with a university goal in mind. A few weeks later, homework takes most of the evening and the first test is disappointing. It is tempting to conclude that the course choice was wrong. Sometimes it is. Sometimes a suitable course has exposed an algebra gap that nobody noticed before.

Before changing AA to AI, or AI to AA, separate those possibilities. A course change affects what the student studies, how they are assessed and potentially which university programmes they can enter. One poor result cannot answer all three questions.

What AA and AI actually emphasise

Analysis and Approaches, usually called AA, gives particular attention to mathematical relationships, algebraic reasoning and developing arguments. Applications and Interpretation, or AI, places greater emphasis on using mathematics to model situations, work with data and interpret results. Both involve reasoning, functions, calculus and technology. Neither is a course where students can avoid thinking mathematically.

The IB mathematics course overview lists AA and AI at both Standard Level and Higher Level. Always compare the complete course name. AI HL and AA SL are different combinations of content, depth and assessment, so simply calling AI the easier option is unhelpful.

Look at the kind of difficulty

Imagine two students struggling with functions. The first can explain a graph but makes errors when rearranging equations. The second carries out calculations accurately but cannot interpret what the graph means in a real situation. They have different needs, even when their test scores match.

For the first student, changing courses may leave the algebra weakness untouched. For the second, moving toward a course with substantial contextual interpretation might introduce more of the skill they find difficult. The useful question is which mathematical tasks suit the learner, alongside which skills can reasonably be improved.

Ask your child to bring one question they found impossible and one they enjoyed. Look beyond the answer. What interested them? Where did they stop? Did they understand the language, recognise the relationship and choose a method?

University requirements come before a switch

Check the published requirements for actual universities and degree programmes under consideration. Requirements vary between institutions and courses, and a general statement that a subject is “accepted for university” is not enough. Ask the school counsellor to help compare the exact mathematics course and level with those requirements.

Keep a written record of what the admissions pages say and when you checked them. If the wording is unclear, ask the university directly. A tutoring provider can help assess mathematical readiness; the school and admissions office should confirm the consequences of a course change.

Try targeted support before deciding

When the course appears suitable but foundations are shaky, agree a short period of focused teaching with a clear review point. For example, repair rearranging equations, function notation and graph interpretation, then try fresh questions from the current unit. Compare independent performance rather than how confident the student feels immediately after a lesson.

If performance improves after the repair, the course may be manageable with better support. If the mathematical demands remain a poor fit, the school can discuss alternatives before the missing content becomes harder to catch up.

What to bring to a consultation

Bring the full course name, examination year, one recent assessment and any university requirements you have identified. Those details make the conversation useful. We can discuss the skills to strengthen and a lesson package that fits the learner’s schedule.

The goal is a decision your child can understand and sustain. A suitable course, realistic study habits and purposeful teaching work together; changing the course name alone does not create that foundation.
