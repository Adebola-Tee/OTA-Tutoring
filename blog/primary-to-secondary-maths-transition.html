Secondary Maths transition

Why Maths Can Feel Harder After Starting Secondary School

By Taiwo Oloyede9 October 20264 min read

A child who enjoyed primary Maths starts secondary school and suddenly says the subject is difficult. Parents can wonder whether the earlier grades were misleading. Often, the change reflects new demands layered onto familiar ideas, not a sudden loss of ability.

Secondary lessons may move between symbols, diagrams and explanations more quickly. Students also have more teachers, a busier timetable and greater responsibility for organising work. The useful response is to identify which part of that transition is causing difficulty.

Familiar numbers can appear in unfamiliar language

A primary pupil might solve a missing-number question such as 7 + □ = 12 confidently. Replacing the box with x does not change the relationship, but the notation may make it feel like a new subject.

Show 7 + x = 12 and ask what x represents. Connect it to the earlier problem before introducing longer equations. Letters are not a signal to abandon everything already learned about equality and operations.

Definitions can also create uncertainty. A learner who can calculate an average may not immediately recognise the instruction find the mean. Ask which terms have changed and practise using them accurately in an explanation.

Check the foundations that new topics assume

Secondary work may combine several earlier skills in one question. A problem involving a percentage of a fraction can expose uncertainty with either idea. A student who is quick with whole numbers may need more work on proportional reasoning.

Consider a rectangle measuring 3.5 units by 2 units. The area is 7 square units. If the learner assumes the result must be a whole-number multiple from a times table, decimals may be the obstacle rather than area itself.

Use one task at a time to locate the gap. Our article on repeated school Maths mistakes explains why the first wrong step is more useful than a general judgement about accuracy.

Make independent working visible

In a new classroom, the child may need to start without the prompts they previously received. At home, ask them to attempt a suitable question before helping. Watch whether they understand the instruction and can choose a first step.

Do not interpret every pause as a problem. Thinking takes time. If they genuinely cannot begin, offer a small prompt and note what was needed. That can inform a conversation with the teacher or tutor.

Help organise the practical side too. Knowing where the homework is recorded, which equipment is needed and when a task is due can reduce confusion that has little to do with mathematical ability.

Expect explanation as well as calculation

A correct answer may need supporting reasoning. Ask why two fractions are equivalent or why an angle relationship applies. Encourage a complete mathematical sentence rather than a list of disconnected numbers.

For example, if corresponding angles are used, the learner should establish the relevant parallel lines. A diagram that merely looks parallel is not enough. This shift from noticing to justifying can take practice.

The EEF's Key Stages 2 and 3 maths guidance is useful for understanding how teaching can connect prior knowledge with new ideas. For an international learner, use their own school's curriculum and terminology to plan support.
