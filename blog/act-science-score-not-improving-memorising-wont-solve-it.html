ACT Science Advice

ACT Science Score Not Improving? Why Memorising Biology and Chemistry Won’t Solve It

By Taiwo Oloyede23 September 20264 min read

When an ACT Science score stalls, students often respond by reviewing more Biology, Chemistry and Physics. That feels logical: the section is called Science.

But if the real weakness is interpreting evidence under time pressure, more memorisation may add knowledge without changing the score.

What ACT Science actually measures

ACT’s official Science test description focuses on interpretation, analysis, evaluation, reasoning and problem-solving in scientific contexts.

Some background knowledge helps, but most questions require students to use information supplied in passages, graphs, tables and experiments. They must locate evidence, compare conditions, recognise relationships and evaluate explanations.

Content review can hide the true problem

A student may spend hours relearning cellular respiration and still lose a question because they read the wrong axis. They may memorise chemical equations but fail to identify which experiment contains the control.

Before assigning another content chapter, classify the errors:

  • misread graph or unit
  • overlooked a condition
  • confused independent and dependent variables
  • unsupported inference
  • difficulty comparing viewpoints
  • time lost through passage reading
  • genuinely missing background knowledge

Only the final category is solved mainly through subject revision.

Scientific vocabulary should be functional

Students do need terms such as control, hypothesis, precision and variable. However, definitions should be practised inside experiments. Ask the student to identify the control in a new design or predict what changing a variable would do.

Knowing a definition without recognising its role is another form of passive learning.

Use the answer choices as claims to test

Rather than asking, “Which option sounds scientifically true?”, ask, “Which option is supported by this evidence?”

An answer can be true in the real world yet wrong for the presented study. Students should point to the figure, trend or passage statement that supports their choice and identify why alternatives fail.

Replace broad revision with skill drills

A more productive week might include:

  1. two graph-comparison drills
  2. one experimental-design passage
  3. one conflicting-models passage
  4. short timed passage sets
  5. delayed correction of recurring errors

After each set, record both accuracy and cause. A score without an error pattern offers little guidance.

When content review is genuinely needed

Targeted review helps when a question assumes a basic concept the student does not understand, such as pH, density, genetic inheritance or forces. Repair that small foundation, then return immediately to applied questions.

Do not turn a specific gap into a complete rereading of a school science textbook.

If visual evidence is the recurring barrier, use this guide to improve ACT Science graph and table interpretation.

Parents can ask their child to justify an answer using only the supplied evidence. If they immediately begin reciting a memorised lesson, gently redirect them to the figure or experiment. That simple distinction often reveals why effort has not produced movement.

Measure reasoning, not pages revised

At the end of each week, choose a small set of unseen passages. Track accuracy within the time limit and count errors by category. The student should also explain two answers aloud by pointing to the exact evidence. This gives families a clearer progress measure than chapters completed or flashcards reviewed.

When a score rises, check why. Was graph navigation faster? Were fewer assumptions made? Did experimental-design questions improve? Naming the successful change helps the student repeat it under pressure.
