GCSE Biology Advice

GCSE Biology Exam Technique: Why Memorising the Textbook Isn’t Enough

By Taiwo Oloyede22 September 20265 min read

Your child has revised cells, enzymes, infection, homeostasis and genetics.

Ask them a question at home and they know the answer.

Then they sit a GCSE Biology paper and somehow lose marks on topics they clearly understand.

This is where many parents assume the solution is more revision.

Often, it is not.

The student may already know enough Biology. What they have not mastered yet is how GCSE Biology questions expect that knowledge to be used.

Knowing Biology is only part of the exam

For AQA GCSE Biology, around 40% of the marks assess knowledge and understanding, 40% assess application, and 20% assess analysis, evaluation and drawing conclusions.

You can see the full breakdown in the official AQA GCSE Biology assessment guidance.

That explains why memorising a revision guide can produce disappointing results.

A student may know how osmosis works. The exam can then place osmosis inside an unfamiliar investigation and ask them to explain the results.

Now they must recognise the Biology themselves and use it correctly.

That is a different skill.

Command words can quietly cost several marks

A child sees a familiar topic and starts writing everything they remember.

But what did the question actually ask?

Describe?

Explain?

Compare?

Suggest?

Evaluate?

These words matter.

For example, AQA defines compare as describing similarities and/or differences. Writing about only one thing may therefore leave the answer incomplete.

Parents and students can check the official AQA GCSE Science command words.

A useful habit is simple:

Before writing, identify the command word and the number of marks available.

That gives the answer direction before the student starts.

“But that’s what I meant” does not earn the mark

This happens constantly in Biology.

The student reads the mark scheme and says:

“That is exactly what I was trying to say.”

The problem is that the examiner cannot mark what the student intended.

Biology answers need precision.

Instead of saying:

“The cells take in more.”

Which cells?

What are they taking in?

By what process?

What happens as a result?

Students should get used to reading their own explanation and asking:

“Would somebody who has never spoken to me know exactly what I mean?”

If the answer is no, the response probably needs more scientific detail.

Practicals deserve proper revision too

Required practicals are easy to underestimate.

A child may remember carrying out an experiment at school but struggle when the exam asks why a variable was controlled or how the method could be improved.

AQA states that questions drawing on practical knowledge and understanding account for at least 15% of GCSE Biology marks.

Students should be comfortable with:

variables methods repeat readings graphs and tables accuracy sources of error conclusions improvements to an investigation

The important part is understanding why each step is carried out.

Biology papers contain more data than many students expect

Graphs, tables, percentages, ratios and magnification calculations can all appear.

A student who knows the biological content may still drop marks because they struggle to interpret what the data is showing.

When a graph appears, encourage your child to pause.

Read both axes.

Check the units.

Look for the overall pattern.

Then answer the question using the actual data.

Students sometimes ignore the information in front of them because they are searching their memory for a textbook sentence.

Mixed questions reveal whether the knowledge is secure

Topic worksheets are useful when a student is learning.

They become less useful if they are the only type of practice being completed.

If the page says Homeostasis Questions, your child already knows which chapter to think about.

The real exam will not always give them that advantage.

As mocks and GCSE exams approach, revision should include mixed questions where your child must recognise for themselves whether a problem involves enzymes, inheritance, transport, ecology or another topic.

That is much closer to what happens in the exam.

Past papers should teach something

Completing a paper, checking the score and immediately opening another paper wastes a lot of useful information.

For every lost mark, ask why it happened.

Was the Biology missing?

Was the command word misunderstood?

Was the explanation too vague?

Was the data interpreted incorrectly?

Was it a practical question?

Was the question simply misread?

Once those patterns become visible, revision becomes much more focused.

What should Biology revision look like now?

Keep the textbook.

Your child still needs the content.

But once a topic has been learned, move quickly into questions.

A useful routine is:

Learn the topic.

Recall it without notes.

Attempt exam questions.

Mark them carefully.

Correct the mistakes.

Return to the same skill later.

This is how knowledge begins turning into marks.
