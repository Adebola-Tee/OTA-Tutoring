Northern Ireland SEAG Preparation

What to practise after receiving your SEAG pupil card

By Taiwo Oloyede10 October 20264 min read

Receiving a SEAG pupil card can make the November assessments suddenly feel close. Parents may respond by adding more papers, while a child begins worrying about every wrong answer. A better next step is to identify what would make the remaining practice more useful.

The official SEAG timeline lists pupil cards from 9 October 2026, followed by assessments on 14 and 21 November. Check, download and print the card, and follow the assessment centre's instructions. Then move from administration to a manageable Maths and English plan.

Review one recent attempt carefully

Choose a practice attempt your child completed with limited help. Keep the original answers and ask them to explain a few correct and incorrect choices. A total score shows an outcome, but the explanation reveals what needs attention.

Separate three situations: the child did not know the idea, misunderstood the question or knew the method but made an execution error. An unfamiliar word, a forgotten fraction method and a rushed subtraction should not all receive the same advice to “be more careful.”

Pick two recurring priorities. A small, clear focus is easier to practise and review than trying to repair every weakness at once.

Teach a Maths decision before adding speed

Here is an original example. A box holds 36 pencils. One third are blue. How many are not blue? First find the blue pencils: 36 ÷ 3 = 12. Then subtract from the total: 36 − 12 = 24.

A child who answers 12 may understand fractions but stop before answering the actual question. Ask them to underline what is requested and label the intermediate number. Then change the fraction and total so that they must repeat the reasoning rather than remember 24.

If the fraction itself is unclear, use a drawing or counters to show equal groups first. If the decision is secure but the arithmetic is slow, practise the relevant number facts separately. Those are different lesson goals.

Make English answers depend on evidence

Try this short original sentence: “Mina gripped the envelope, took a breath and approached the headteacher's door.” Ask what suggests that Mina might be nervous. The actions give clues; the sentence does not tell us what is inside the envelope.

Discuss why an answer such as “she has received bad results” goes beyond the evidence. A plausible story is not automatically a supported inference. Ask the child to point to a clue and connect it to their choice.

For vocabulary, read the whole sentence before guessing a word's meaning. Our guide to SEAG comprehension tutoring gives further ways to practise evidence and inference without turning reading into a memory exercise.

Build a weekly routine your child can sustain

Use short sessions to teach and revisit the two priorities, alongside ordinary reading and mixed questions. Include timed practice when the child can apply the relevant methods accurately. Speed should grow from understanding and sensible decisions, rather than pressure to rush.

Leave time to review a paper after completing it. If your child remembers only the score and never revisits the mistakes, the next paper may repeat the same pattern. Agree one specific improvement before the next attempt, such as identifying the final quantity in word problems.

Keep breaks and enjoyable activities in the week. The card confirms assessment details; it does not mean every spare minute must become revision.

Measure independence as well as marks

After a few sessions, revisit a priority using fresh questions. Does your child need fewer hints? Can they explain why an answer fits? Are they moving on from a difficult item more calmly?

Those changes help you decide whether the practice is working. Compare several attempts rather than treating one good or difficult afternoon as a prediction of the assessment outcome. If progress stalls, change the teaching or task before simply increasing the workload.
