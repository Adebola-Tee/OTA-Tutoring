GCSE Maths Exam Skills

How to use GCSE Maths formula sheets in unfamiliar questions

By Taiwo Oloyede10 October 20264 min read

A GCSE Maths formula sheet can be reassuring until your child meets a question that does not name the formula they need. The sheet supplies information. The student still has to recognise the situation, choose the right relationship and decide what each value represents.

For 2027, formula sheets are confirmed for GCSE Maths under Ofqual's published arrangements. Use the official sheet for your child's board and tier when practising. Check the school's instructions for mocks, because a school assessment is not automatically identical to the final examination.

Read the shape before reading the numbers

Try this original example. A trapezium has parallel sides of 8 cm and 14 cm. Its perpendicular height is 5 cm. What is its area?

The formula is half the sum of the parallel sides multiplied by the height. The calculation is ½ × (8 + 14) × 5 = 55 cm². Before substituting, ask the learner to identify the two parallel sides and the perpendicular distance between them.

A sloping edge is not automatically the height. If a diagram contains an extra measurement, the student must decide whether it belongs in the formula. Substituting every visible number is not a reliable method, even when the correct formula is available.

Change the unknown to check understanding

Now keep the area at 55 cm² and the parallel sides at 8 cm and 14 cm, but ask for the height. The same formula becomes 55 = 11 × h, so h = 5 cm.

The question has changed from calculating an area to finding a missing length. A learner who can only use the formula in its original arrangement may pause here. Practise rearranging through manageable equations and explain why each operation keeps the two sides equal.

Avoid jumping straight to a complicated rearrangement if the basic relationship is unclear. Start with what is known, what is unknown and which equation connects them. The formula sheet cannot make those decisions for the student.

Use units to expose a hidden mistake

Suppose another trapezium has parallel sides of 0.8 m and 1.4 m, with a perpendicular height of 50 cm. The lengths need consistent units before substitution. Using metres gives a height of 0.5 m and an area of 0.55 m².

Using centimetres instead gives sides of 80 cm and 140 cm, so the area is 5,500 cm². One square metre contains 10,000 square centimetres. This is a useful moment to check area conversion rather than applying the ordinary length conversion once.

Ask your child to explain why the two answers describe the same area. If they cannot, more formula recall is unlikely to fix the underlying unit difficulty.

Check whether the answer fits the diagram

For the first trapezium, a rectangle 8 cm wide and 5 cm high has area 40 cm². One 14 cm wide and 5 cm high has area 70 cm². An area of 55 cm² lies between those values, which supports the result.

Estimation does not replace working, but it can catch an answer that is far too large or small. Build that habit before using a calculator to produce a precise decimal. A correct numerical output is only useful if it answers the question asked.

Practise choosing without a chapter heading

A worksheet labelled “trapezium area” tells the student which method to use. Later, mix area tasks with perimeter, missing lengths and other topics appropriate to their tier. Ask them to explain the choice before calculating.

Record whether an error came from recognising the shape, choosing a measurement, rearranging or converting units. Our guide to GCSE Higher Maths before mocks explains how those specific errors can shape a useful lesson plan.

Bring an unfamiliar question to the first conversation

If your child knows the formulas but cannot start new questions, bring an example with their original working. Tell us the examination board, tier and the topics their school is currently teaching.
