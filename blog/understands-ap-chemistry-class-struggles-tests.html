AP Chemistry Advice

My Child Understands AP Chemistry in Class but Struggles on Tests

By Taiwo Oloyede23 September 20264 min read

A student can genuinely understand AP Chemistry in class and still struggle on tests. During a lesson, the topic is known, the teacher guides the sequence and each completed step makes sense.

During an assessment, the student must identify the chemistry, retrieve the relevant model and construct the reasoning independently. These are different demands.

Recognition feels like mastery

Worked examples are easy to follow because the route has already been chosen. Students may recognise why each step is correct without being able to generate that route from a blank page.

After studying an example, close it and attempt a similar question. Then move to a question with unfamiliar wording or data. The second transition reveals whether learning is transferable.

The test combines topics

Classwork is often organised by unit. A test may combine stoichiometry, thermodynamics, equilibrium and particle-level reasoning in one problem.

Students who rely on chapter labels may not recognise which principles apply. Mixed practice should therefore begin before final review.

Notes are retrieved, but evidence is not used

An answer may contain correct Chemistry yet ignore the graph, table or experimental observation supplied in the question.

Use the structure:

  1. make the chemical claim
  2. cite the relevant evidence
  3. explain the particle-level or quantitative reason

This keeps the response connected to the specific prompt.

Test anxiety may reveal an unstable method

Pressure can make any student less efficient, but a method that depends on recognising familiar wording is especially fragile.

Build a consistent approach: identify the task, represent the particles or process, state the governing principle, solve and check the chemical meaning.

Short timed sets can develop this routine without the burden of a full practice exam.

Corrections are understood but not retained

When a teacher explains the missed question, the solution may seem obvious. The student should not stop there.

They should close the correction, reproduce the reasoning and later answer a different question testing the same skill. The later attempt is evidence that learning transferred.

Written explanations need precision

Students often say the right idea aloud but write an incomplete response. Terms such as bond, force, interaction, energy, concentration and rate must be used accurately.

Require the student to connect cause and effect rather than list keywords.

College Board states that the AP Chemistry exam assesses course concepts and the ability to design and describe chemical experiments. Test preparation should therefore include reasoning, data and experimental communication.

Use a post-test diagnosis

Sort lost points into:

  • content not recalled
  • unfamiliar context not recognised
  • calculation or unit error
  • data or experimental evidence ignored
  • explanation incomplete
  • timing or anxiety-related decision

Do not prescribe more content review when the dominant issue is application or communication.

If FRQs are the main source of lost points, read why strong students lose points on AP Chemistry free-response questions.

Change the final stage of homework

Students often finish homework after checking that every answer is complete. Add one more stage: choose two questions and explain the solution from memory without looking at the written work.

Then alter one feature of the problem. Change the concentration, particle interaction, temperature or experimental result and predict how the reasoning would change.

This small variation forces transfer rather than repetition. It also shows whether the student understood the governing principle or followed the surface pattern of the example.

Once a week, complete a short cumulative set containing older units. Regular mixing reduces dependence on chapter labels and makes test questions feel less surprising.
