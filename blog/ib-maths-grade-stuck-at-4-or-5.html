IB Maths Grades

How to Improve an IB Maths Grade Stuck at 4 or 5

By Taiwo Oloyede6 October 20264 min read

A grade of 4 or 5 can be especially frustrating. Your child knows plenty of the course, works regularly and sometimes solves difficult questions. Yet the overall result barely moves. More revision feels like the obvious answer, but the missing marks may be concentrated in a few specific decisions.

To move forward, find out how those marks are being lost. The grade itself is too broad to tell you whether the priority is algebra, interpretation, unfamiliar questions or written reasoning.

Sort the lost marks into useful groups

Take a marked test and separate mistakes into four groups. The first is missing knowledge: the student did not understand the underlying idea. The second is a method error: they recognised the task but used a technique incorrectly. The third is a decision error: they chose the wrong route. The fourth is incomplete communication: useful thinking never became a clear written answer.

Use the groups as a guide, not a rigid diagnosis. One question can contain more than one problem. The point is to stop treating every incorrect answer as another topic to reread.

Distinguish secure knowledge from familiarity

Suppose a learner can solve an equation while looking at yesterday’s example. That is encouraging, but it does not establish independent fluency. Can they solve a rearranged version next week? Can they recognise the same relationship inside a worded problem?

Students often overestimate learning when an answer looks familiar. Replace some rereading with closed-book attempts, followed by correction and a later question. A shorter practice set can be more revealing than an evening spent scanning notes.

Course requirements matter here. The IB mathematics overview distinguishes AA and AI at SL and HL. An improvement plan should match the actual course and examination year, rather than borrowing a generic “IB Maths” checklist.

Find marks that are repeatedly within reach

A learner stuck at 4 or 5 does not necessarily need to begin with the hardest question in the paper. First identify accessible marks lost repeatedly through weak execution. These might involve accurate substitution, reading graph scales, interpreting calculator output or showing a required justification.

For example, a student may correctly identify a stationary point but fail to explain its nature. Another may fit a statistical model and then give a number without saying what it means in the context. These are teachable habits, but each needs a different lesson.

Improvement is more likely when the student understands why their original answer was insufficient. Copying the completed solution does not provide that explanation.

Give the next fortnight one clear purpose

Choose a pair of priorities from the test. One might be an underlying skill, such as rearranging expressions. The other might be an exam habit, such as linking a conclusion to mathematical evidence.

Practise the skill in short sets, then use it inside mixed questions. Keep one or two examples in an error record, alongside the corrected reasoning. At the end of the fortnight, attempt fresh questions that require the same decisions.

The review should ask whether the repeated error has reduced, not simply whether more work has been completed. Our article on why IB Math AA past papers may not improve scores explains the difference between paper completion and useful correction.
