Maths Learning Advice

How to Help a Child Who Has Lost Confidence in Maths

By Taiwo Oloyede20 September 20264 min read

Sometimes the biggest problem is no longer the Maths.

It is the sentence your child has started believing:

“I’m just bad at Maths.”

Once that belief settles in, even questions they could normally answer can start to feel impossible.

They hesitate.

They second-guess themselves.

They avoid homework.

And after a while, every difficult question seems to prove what they already think about themselves.

The good news is that Maths confidence can be rebuilt.

But usually not by simply telling a child:

“You can do it.”

They need to start experiencing evidence that they actually can.

Start with something they can succeed at

If your child has completely lost confidence, do not begin with the hardest topic they are struggling with.

Start slightly below their current difficulty level.

Give them questions they can complete successfully, then gradually increase the challenge.

This is not “making the work too easy”.

It is rebuilding momentum.

A student who has failed repeatedly may need a few genuine wins before they are ready to tackle harder work again.

Stop using one bad result as proof

A poor Maths test tells you what happened on one test.

It does not tell you what your child is capable of becoming.

Instead of saying:

“You only got 35% again.”

Try asking:

“Which questions did you understand, and where did things start going wrong?”

That changes the conversation from judgement to problem-solving.

Find the exact point where confidence broke

Children rarely lose confidence in Maths for no reason.

Sometimes it begins with one topic.

Fractions.

Algebra.

Long division.

Word problems.

Then the child falls behind, lessons move on, and suddenly Maths feels like a subject they cannot understand anymore.

Go backwards.

Find the earliest point where the gaps began.

Fixing that one missing foundation can make later topics feel far less intimidating.

Do not correct every mistake immediately

This can be difficult for parents.

Your child makes an error and you instantly want to say:

“No, that’s wrong.”

Instead, try:

“Have another look at that step.”

or:

“What were you trying to do here?”

Let them find the error where possible.

Constant correction can make an already nervous child afraid to attempt anything without checking first.

The goal is to build independence, not dependence on reassurance.

Praise the process, not just the answer

Instead of only saying:

“Well done, you got it right.”

Notice what actually improved.

For example:

“You didn’t give up when that question looked difficult.”

“You checked your working and found the mistake yourself.”

“You remembered the method without looking at your notes.”

Those are the habits that eventually improve grades.

Avoid turning Maths into a daily battle

If every evening ends with arguments over homework, both parent and child begin to dread the subject.

Shorter sessions can work better.

Twenty focused minutes where your child is calm and thinking is often more useful than an hour where everyone is frustrated.

Consistency matters more than forcing huge amounts of work.

Watch how adults talk about Maths

Children hear more than we realise.

Comments such as:

“I was terrible at Maths too.”

or:

“Maths just isn't our thing.”

may sound harmless, but a struggling child can easily hear:

“This is something people in our family simply cannot do.”

Try to keep the language neutral.

Maths is a skill.

Skills improve with the right teaching and enough practice.

When extra support can help

Sometimes confidence returns once the academic gap is properly addressed.

A tutor may help when your child:

avoids asking questions in class has several gaps from earlier years has stopped believing they can improve becomes upset before Maths tests repeatedly says they understand nothing needs concepts explained at a different pace

Our guide on when parents should consider hiring a GCSE tutor may help you decide whether outside support would be useful.

Confidence comes after evidence

This is the part parents often miss.

You cannot talk a child into Maths confidence.

Confidence usually comes after they begin seeing:

“I understood that.”

“I got that question right.”

“I improved my score.”

“I can actually do this.”

So focus less on making them feel confident immediately.

Help them become more capable.

Confidence often follows.
