AP Biology Advice

My Child Knows AP Biology Content but Cannot Score Well on FRQs — Why?

By Taiwo Oloyede23 September 20264 min read

It can be baffling when a student explains photosynthesis or natural selection confidently at home, then earns far fewer points than expected on an AP Biology free-response question. Parents often assume the content was not learned properly. Sometimes that is true, but frequently the real problem is translation: the student knows the Biology yet does not express it in the form the question rewards.

AP Biology FRQs test more than recall

The free-response section asks students to explain processes, analyse representations, use experimental evidence, perform calculations and justify claims. According to the official AP Biology exam overview, six FRQs make up half of the examination score.

That means knowledge alone is not enough. A strong answer must select the relevant idea and use it precisely.

The command word may be controlling the mark

Students often treat describe, explain, predict and justify as if they mean the same thing.

  • Describe states an observable pattern or feature.
  • Explain gives a biological reason or mechanism.
  • Predict states the expected outcome.
  • Justify connects the prediction or claim to evidence and Biology.

If the question asks for a justification and the student merely repeats the prediction, the answer can sound sensible while missing the point.

Vague Biology loses precise points

Phrases such as “it helps the organism”, “the cells work harder” or “the level changes” rarely show enough. The student should name the variable, direction and mechanism: what increases or decreases, why it changes and how that affects the system.

This is particularly important in questions about cell communication, gene expression, energy transfer and evolution. Mark-worthy reasoning is usually a short chain, not a long paragraph.

Data must appear inside the reasoning

Some students understand a graph but never quote or compare its values. Others copy numbers without explaining what they demonstrate. A useful structure is:

  1. State the pattern.
  2. Support it with a relevant comparison from the data.
  3. Connect that pattern to the biological claim.

Units, labels and experimental groups matter. Evidence should be chosen, not dumped into the response.

More writing is not automatically better

Long introductions and rewritten questions consume time. They can also introduce contradictions. Students should answer each labelled part directly, using complete but economical sentences.

A good correction exercise is to highlight the exact clause that earns each point. If no phrase can be linked to the demand, the response probably contains knowledge without a scored answer.

What should your child practise this week?

Choose three released FRQ parts rather than completing an entire paper. For each one:

  1. circle the command word
  2. underline the evidence or variable provided
  3. write a direct answer under timed conditions
  4. compare it with the scoring guidance
  5. rewrite only the missing reasoning
  6. attempt a similar question two days later

Track whether lost points came from content, interpretation, weak evidence, vague language or incomplete justification. That diagnosis is more useful than recording one total score.

If data-heavy prompts are the main weakness, continue with why students lose points on AP Biology data-analysis questions.

Parents do not need to reteach the syllabus. Ask your child to show exactly why each corrected sentence would earn a point. If they cannot explain that, they may still be copying rather than learning the method.

Keep this review specific. One precise improvement—such as supporting every claim with a data comparison—is easier to practise and measure than a vague instruction to “write better answers”.
