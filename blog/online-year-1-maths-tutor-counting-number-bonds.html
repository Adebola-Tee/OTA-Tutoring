Online Year 1 Maths Tutor: Counting, Number Bonds and a Confident Start

By Taiwo Oloyede1 October 20264 min read

An online Year 1 Maths tutor should make numbers understandable for a young child, not turn every session into a worksheet test. A child may recite numbers confidently while still finding it difficult to count objects accurately, compare quantities or explain a simple addition.

The first step is to find out what the child can do with real objects and spoken questions. Their school work is useful, but one unfinished page does not tell the whole story. Ask what the class is learning, what the child enjoys and when the work becomes frustrating.

Check counting in three different ways

Listen to the child count, then ask them to count a small collection of objects. Do they give each object one number name? Do they know that the last number tells us how many are in the collection?

Move the same objects into a different arrangement and ask whether the total changed. If the child recounts every time, explore the idea through small examples rather than treating it as a failure. Five counters remain five when spread out, provided none were added or removed.

Next, begin counting from a number other than one. A child who can continue from seven has a useful starting point for counting on in addition. Keep the task short enough to notice their strategy, not just their stamina.

Connect the objects, picture and number sentence

England's Year 1 Maths programme includes counting, addition and subtraction, and number bonds within twenty. Schools sequence learning differently, so ask which range and representations the child currently uses.

For a small addition, begin with two groups of counters. Combine them, draw what happened and write the matching number sentence. Ask the child to point out where each number appears in the model.

Then change the task. If there are eight counters altogether and three are in one group, how many are in the other? This introduces a missing part through something visible. The symbols should record an understood relationship rather than appear as an unexplained rule.

Build number bonds without a speed contest

Use a familiar total such as five or ten. Split a collection in different ways and describe the two parts. Let the child predict a missing part, then check with the objects.

Over time, ask whether an earlier fact helps. Knowing that seven and three make ten can support finding what must be added to seven. The aim is a dependable relationship between quantities, followed by increasingly fluent recall.

Avoid presenting every hesitation as a need to work faster. First check whether the child understands the task and has a useful way to solve it. Speed without understanding can disguise guessing or make a willing learner reluctant to try.

Make an online lesson active and manageable

A few safe household objects, paper and a pencil can support a lesson. Agree the setup beforehand so the parent is not searching for materials while the child waits. Small children may need an adult nearby for practical help and attention.

Alternate looking, moving objects, drawing and explaining. A teacher can show a model, but the learner should also make their own. Ask brief questions that reveal a decision: which group has more, what changed, or how could we check?

Discuss an appropriate session length and breaks rather than assuming a young child should follow an older student's routine. The best arrangement is one in which the child participates and the parent understands the practice task afterwards.

Look for small, observable progress

Useful signs include counting objects accurately, recognising a quantity in a new arrangement and choosing a strategy without being told every step. Keep examples of independent attempts so progress does not depend only on an impression after a cheerful lesson.

Home practice can be a brief game, such as finding two groups that make ten. Stop while the activity still feels manageable. For the next stage, our Year 3 Maths tutoring guide shows how early number understanding supports later work.
