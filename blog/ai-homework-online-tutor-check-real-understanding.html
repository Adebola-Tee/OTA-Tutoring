AI Homework Help: How Parents Can Check What Their Child Actually Understands

By Taiwo Oloyede30 September 20264 min read

A polished homework answer does not always show what a child understands. With AI tools able to generate explanations, parents may see complete work while the student still cannot begin a similar question alone. If you are comparing AI homework help with an online tutor, start by looking at the learning that happens after the answer appears.

The aim is not to turn every homework conversation into an investigation. It is to find out whether your child can explain a decision, spot a mistake and use the idea again. Those checks work whether the help came from a tool, a friend, a parent or a worked example.

Ask for the first step, not the whole solution

Choose one question and ask your child what it wants them to find. Then ask what they would do first and why. Give them time to think. A learner may understand an idea but find it difficult to explain immediately.

For a Maths question, change a number or the context. If they can still choose a suitable method, the original explanation may have helped. If they search for an identical answer, return to the missing concept rather than assigning another full worksheet.

For English, ask why a piece of evidence supports the point. If the response uses vocabulary the student cannot explain, invite them to rewrite it in their own words. Clearer language often reveals what they actually mean.

Separate assistance from authorship

Agree what help is allowed by the school. A request for a hint, a definition or feedback may be treated differently from submitting a generated response. School rules and the requirements of a particular assignment should guide that choice.

UNESCO's guidance on generative AI in education emphasises a human centred approach to using these tools. For a family, a practical version of that principle is keeping the learner's thinking visible instead of letting a tool replace it.

Ask the child to record the part they attempted independently and the point where they needed help. This gives a teacher or tutor useful information. It also makes asking for help more specific than saying the entire subject is difficult.

A three question check after homework

Try three calm questions: what did you learn, where did you need help, and can you show it on a new example? These should be a short discussion rather than a nightly oral examination.

For Science, a learner might explain a graph trend and then apply the same reasoning to a different graph. Ask which data supports the conclusion. Correct scientific vocabulary is valuable, but it should connect to an idea the learner can describe.

If a child cannot answer, that is a starting point for teaching. Avoid treating every hesitation as proof of cheating. They may be tired, unfamiliar with explaining their reasoning or missing an earlier foundation.

What a human tutor should do differently

An online homework tutor should notice the point where the learner becomes uncertain, explain it at the right level and check a fresh attempt. Simply completing the assignment on screen leaves the original difficulty intact.

A useful session might take one attempted question, uncover the missing step and practise two variations. The student then returns to the homework with a method they can use. This protects their ownership of the work while making the next independent attempt more achievable.

We would expect the prompts to reduce over time. If the tutor still supplies every decision after several sessions, ask whether the goal needs to change. Finished homework is helpful, but independence is the better sign of progress.

Choose support around a specific need

Bring the school instructions, your child's attempt and one similar question they could not solve. Ask the tutor to explain what support is appropriate and how they will check learning. This is more useful than a broad promise to make homework effortless.
