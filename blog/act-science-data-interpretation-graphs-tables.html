ACT Science Data Interpretation: Why Graphs and Tables Cause Students Problems

By Taiwo Oloyede23 September 20264 min read

Many ACT Science mistakes begin before the student performs any reasoning. They look at the right graph, read the wrong line and select a perfectly logical answer to a question that was never asked.

Graphs and tables are not difficult only because they contain data. They compress several decisions into a small space, and each decision must be accurate.

The student skips the labels

Axes, units, legends and headings tell the student what a number means. A value of 20 might represent seconds, degrees, concentration or number of organisms. Without the label, it is not evidence.

Use a brief routine before answering:

  1. identify the requested figure or table
  2. read both axes or column headings
  3. check units and scale
  4. locate the correct group or symbol
  5. trace carefully to the requested value

The routine is short enough for a timed test and prevents avoidable mistakes.

Unusual scales are overlooked

Not every axis begins at zero or increases by one. Students should check intervals, broken axes and logarithmic scales rather than assuming equal-looking distances represent familiar changes.

When the options are close, an incorrect scale assumption can decide the answer.

Students look for exact values when a trend is enough

Some questions ask whether a quantity increases, decreases or remains approximately constant. Estimation can be faster and safer than forcing an exact reading from a plotted point.

Other questions do require interpolation or a calculation. The skill is recognising which level of precision the prompt demands.

Two variables are compared incompletely

Students may follow one line correctly but forget that the question asks how it differs from another condition. Underline the comparison words: greater than, least, between, as temperature increases or at the same time.

Then state both sides of the comparison mentally before choosing an option.

Outside knowledge overrides the presented data

If a graph conflicts with what the student expected from class, the answer must still follow the evidence unless the question explicitly asks for external knowledge. ACT Science tests whether students can work with the scientific information provided.

The official ACT score guidance identifies Interpretation of Data as a major Science reporting category, alongside Scientific Investigation and Evaluation of Models and Results.

Practise micro-skills before full sections

Create short drills that isolate one decision:

  • locate values with units
  • compare two conditions
  • identify maximum, minimum and intersection
  • calculate a simple difference or rate
  • predict a continuation of a trend
  • combine information from a table and graph

Require the student to point to the exact evidence before checking the answer. Accuracy should stabilise before timing becomes tighter.

If graphs are accurate but the whole section remains unfinished, read what to fix when a student runs out of time on ACT Science.

Parents can help with a simple question: “Show me where the graph says that.” If the student cannot trace the answer back to a label, value or trend, the reasoning is not yet secure.

Improvement should be measured by error type. Fewer wrong-line, wrong-unit and wrong-scale errors are meaningful progress even before the overall practice score jumps.

When accuracy becomes reliable, add timing in small steps. First limit the time spent locating evidence, then time whole questions and finally combine passages. This protects the reading routine while building speed, instead of encouraging the student to skip labels and hope for the best.
