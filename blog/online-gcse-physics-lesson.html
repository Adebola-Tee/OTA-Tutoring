Online GCSE Physics lessons

What happens in an online GCSE Physics lesson

By Taiwo Oloyede19 October 20264 min read

Online GCSE Physics lessons should help the learner connect an idea with an explanation, a diagram and an appropriate calculation. If the session consists entirely of watching the tutor substitute numbers, a student may leave with completed notes and the same difficulty starting alone.

At OTA Learning Studio, paid sessions take place through Google Meet. In a free parent consultation, we discuss the course, current difficulties and practical setup before agreeing available lesson times and fees. This example illustrates a lesson on circuits; the student's work determines the actual starting point.

Establish what the learner thinks is happening

Consider a 6-volt supply connected across a 3-ohm resistor in a simple model. Ask what voltage, current and resistance represent before asking for a calculation. Current is not something the resistor simply uses up.

Using V = IR gives I = V/R, so the current is 2 amperes. Ask the student to explain the rearrangement and the meaning of the result. If the calculation is correct but the explanation is uncertain, the concept still deserves attention.

A diagram can help make the model explicit. The tutor should check that the learner recognises the components and understands the assumptions of the example rather than treating it as a set of unrelated letters.

Change one feature and make a prediction

Keep the voltage at 6 volts and increase the resistance to 6 ohms. Ask whether the current should increase or decrease before calculating. It becomes 1 ampere, consistent with the inverse relationship when voltage is fixed.

Now increase the voltage with resistance unchanged. Can the learner explain the different relationship? Predicting before calculating gives the student a way to question an implausible result.

These are idealised teaching examples, not instructions for a home electrical experiment. Real components can behave differently under changing conditions, and practical work needs the school's appropriate equipment and supervision.

Include a practical reasoning task

A lesson can discuss a supplied method for investigating current and voltage. Ask which quantity is changed, which is measured and what needs controlling for a fair comparison.

The student might examine a data table or a graph and explain what evidence supports a relationship. A statement such as it is a straight line needs detail: whether the line passes through the origin matters when claiming direct proportionality.

AQA's GCSE Physics specification overview identifies the separate qualification and its topics. Materials should follow the student's actual board and course; Combined Science preparation needs its own check.

Give the learner an independent response

After teaching, provide a new situation and allow time to choose the equation, calculate and explain. The tutor needs to see working, including crossed-out attempts, to identify where reasoning changes.

For a written task, ask why a particular current prediction follows from the fixed quantities. Feedback should address missing scientific links rather than merely add keywords. A brief complete explanation is more useful than an impressive term placed in the wrong context.

Our guide to choosing a Physics tutor explains how this active role helps parents assess the teaching offered. The student should not depend on an adult naming every first step.

Finish with a clear next task

A small practice activity can revisit the idea later using different values or a different representation. The next session should respond to that evidence. A correct answer immediately after a demonstration is encouraging, but a later independent explanation shows more.

Parents should understand both what happens on screen and how the learning will be checked afterwards. The aim is a student who can connect the Physics with the calculation when the next question looks unfamiliar.
