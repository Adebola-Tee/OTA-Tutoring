GCSE Physics Advice

Electricity, Forces and Energy: Why GCSE Physics Suddenly Becomes Difficult

By Taiwo Oloyede21 September 20265 min read

There is a point where many students suddenly say:

“Physics used to make sense. Now I don’t understand anything.”

Very often, that point arrives around Electricity, Forces and Energy.

These topics are not difficult simply because there is more content to remember.

They are difficult because GCSE Physics starts asking students to do several things at once:

understand the science, choose the right equation, use Maths correctly and apply everything to an unfamiliar situation.

That is a big jump.

Why does Electricity feel so confusing?

Electricity introduces several quantities that students must keep separate:

current potential difference resistance power charge energy transferred

The problem is that these ideas are connected.

A student may know what resistance means but then face a question requiring them to choose between equations involving current, voltage, resistance or power.

Under the AQA GCSE Physics specification, for example, students are expected to understand relationships such as current, potential difference and resistance as well as electrical power and energy transfer. See the official AQA GCSE Physics content.

This is where simple memorisation starts breaking down.

Your child needs to understand what each quantity means and how the quantities relate to each other.

Forces brings Physics and Maths together

Forces can look straightforward at first.

Push.

Pull.

Weight.

Friction.

Then students meet:

resultant forces acceleration momentum stopping distances springs work done * force–extension graphs

Now they need much stronger mathematical thinking.

They may have to interpret vectors, rearrange equations, calculate work done or explain what a graph means.

For example, AQA expects students to connect force and displacement through work done and to understand how forces relate to energy transfers.

That means Forces is no longer a topic you can revise by simply memorising definitions.

Students have to solve problems.

Energy is everywhere — and that is part of the problem

Students often learn Energy early and assume they understand it.

Then it keeps returning.

Kinetic energy.

Gravitational potential energy.

Elastic potential energy.

Work done.

Power.

Efficiency.

Energy transfer in electrical devices.

AQA even notes that Paper 2 questions can draw on knowledge of energy transfers and conservation from the earlier Energy and Electricity topics.

So if Energy is weak, the problem does not stay neatly inside one chapter.

It follows the student into other parts of Physics.

The real difficulty: topics start connecting

This is where GCSE Physics feels different from earlier Science.

A question may appear to be about a moving car.

But to solve it, your child might need:

Forces + Energy + an equation + unit conversion + graph interpretation

That is why a student can say:

“But I revised Forces!”

and still struggle.

They may know the topic in isolation but not know how to connect it to other Physics ideas.

Higher grades increasingly depend on making those connections.

Weak Maths becomes much more obvious

Electricity, Forces and Energy expose mathematical weaknesses very quickly.

Students may understand the Physics but still lose marks because they cannot:

rearrange an equation work with decimals convert units use standard form interpret a graph recognise proportional relationships

If your child frequently knows the equation but still gets calculation questions wrong, our guide on why GCSE Physics equations still cost students marks explains what is usually going wrong.

Sometimes the quickest way to improve Physics is actually to strengthen the Maths underneath it.

How should your child revise these topics?

Not by reading the whole chapter again.

Try this instead.

Choose one small area, such as electrical resistance or work done.

Then move through four stages:

1. Explain the idea without notes.

Can your child tell you what it actually means?

2. Use the equation.

Can they calculate the missing quantity?

3. Change the question.

Can they rearrange the same equation when a different quantity is missing?

4. Attempt an exam question.

Can they recognise the topic when nobody tells them which method to use?

That final step is where real understanding shows.

Watch for this sentence

“I understand it when someone explains it, but I can’t do the question myself.”

That usually means your child has reached the point between recognition and independent application.

They do not necessarily need to start Physics again from the beginning.

They need targeted practice turning what they already know into answers.

If this pattern keeps appearing across Physics tests, read 7 signs your child may need a GCSE Physics tutor.

Physics has not suddenly become impossible

The subject has simply become more connected.

Electricity, Forces and Energy require students to move beyond remembering individual facts and start thinking like problem-solvers.

Once those connections become clearer, Physics often starts making sense again.
